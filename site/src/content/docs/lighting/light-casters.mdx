Every light so far has been the same kind of light: a point in space, shining equally in
all directions, as strongly at the far end of the scene as right next to it. That is a
useful fiction for learning the shading terms, and it matches almost nothing real.

Real lights differ in two ways that matter to shading: **which direction the light arrives
from** at a given point, and **how much of it arrives**. This chapter handles the three
standard answers — directional, point and spot — and none of them touches the Phong terms
from 2.2. They only change the two inputs those terms take.

## Directional lights

The sun is 150 million kilometres away. By the time its light reaches a scene, every ray is
effectively parallel: the light arrives from the same direction at every point, and it is
equally strong everywhere.

So a directional light has a **direction** and no position:

```slang title="code/src/2.lighting/light_casters/light_casters.slang"
case kDirectional:
    to_light = normalize(-light.direction.xyz);
    break;
```

The direction stored is the way the light *travels*, which is the convention most engines
use. The lighting terms want the direction *toward* the light, so it is negated. It is also
the same for every fragment — nothing here depends on `input.world_position` — which is
exactly what "infinitely far away" means.

<figure>

Look at the near crates and the far ones: the faces pointing up and to the right are lit to
the same brightness regardless of distance. That uniformity is the signature of a
directional light, and it is why outdoor scenes use one for the sun.

## Point lights and attenuation

A bulb is somewhere. Light leaves it in every direction, so the direction toward it differs
at every point in the scene — which 2.2 already handled — and it **weakens with distance**,
which nothing so far has.

Physically, the same light spreads over the surface of an ever-larger sphere, so intensity
falls with the square of the distance. Pure inverse-square looks wrong in practice, though:
it blows up close to the light and dies off too abruptly for scenes without the rest of the
real-world light transport. The traditional compromise has three terms:

```
intensity = 1 / (constant + linear·d + quadratic·d²)
```

```slang title="code/src/2.lighting/light_casters/light_casters.slang"
float3 offset = light.position.xyz - input.world_position;
float distance = length(offset);
to_light = offset / distance;
intensity = 1.0 / (light.constant_term + light.linear_term * distance +
                   light.quadratic_term * distance * distance);
```

Each term earns its place:

- **constant**, kept at 1.0, stops the denominator dropping below 1. Without it, a surface
  half a unit from the light would receive many times more light than the light emits.
- **linear** controls the gentle falloff at short range.
- **quadratic** takes over at distance and brings the light down toward zero.

The sample uses `1.0`, `0.09` and `0.032`:

| Distance | 1 | 5 | 10 | 16 | 20 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Intensity | 0.89 | 0.44 | 0.20 | 0.09 | 0.06 |

Values like these are usually taken from a table of distances rather than derived. The
thing to understand is the shape: a plateau near the light, then a long tail.

## Spotlights

A torch is a point light that only shines inside a cone. It has a position, it attenuates,
and it also has an **axis** and an **angle**.

The test for "inside the cone" is a dot product. The cosine of the angle between the
spotlight's axis and the ray from the light to this fragment is:

```slang title="code/src/2.lighting/light_casters/light_casters.slang"
float theta = dot(-to_light, normalize(light.direction.xyz));
```

It is 1.0 on the axis and shrinks as the fragment moves off-axis. Comparing it against the
cosine of the cone's half-angle tells you whether the fragment is inside.

A single cutoff gives a pool of light with a razor-sharp edge, which looks like a stage
effect rather than a torch. Two cutoffs give a soft one: full strength inside the **inner**
cone, nothing outside the **outer** cone, and a ramp between:

```slang title="code/src/2.lighting/light_casters/light_casters.slang"
float edge = light.inner_cutoff - light.outer_cutoff;
float cone = saturate((theta - light.outer_cutoff) / edge);
intensity = attenuation * cone;
```

:::caution[Cosines run backwards]
The cutoffs are stored as cosines — computed once on the CPU, so the shader never calls
`acos` per fragment. Cosines *decrease* as angles grow, so the **inner** cutoff, the smaller
angle, is the **larger** number. The subtraction `inner - outer` is positive for exactly
that reason. Swap them and `edge` goes negative and the cone turns inside out.
:::

The sample's torch is held by the camera: its position and direction are rebuilt from the
camera every frame, so the pool of light follows your gaze. In the screenshot at the top,
the soft edge is visible sweeping across the plank face of the crate to the left of centre.

## Ambient is not attenuated

One line deserves a comment:

```slang title="code/src/2.lighting/light_casters/light_casters.slang"
return float4(ambient + intensity * (diffuse + specular), 1.0);
```

The intensity multiplies diffuse and specular, **not ambient**. The ambient term stands for
light that has bounced around the whole scene, and that light does not come from the torch's
direction or care how far away the torch is. It is why the crates outside the cone are dim
rather than invisible.

## One struct for every kind

All three kinds share one `Light` struct:

```slang title="code/src/2.lighting/light_casters/light_casters.slang"
struct Light {
    float4 position;   // xyz: point and spot
    float4 direction;  // xyz: directional and spot
    float4 ambient;
    float4 diffuse;
    float4 specular;
    float constant_term;
    float linear_term;
    float quadratic_term;
    float inner_cutoff;
    float outer_cutoff;
};
```

A directional light ignores `position` and the falloff; a point light ignores `direction` and
the cone. Carrying unused fields costs a few bytes, and buys one layout — which is what lets
chapter 2.6 put lights of different kinds in a single array.

The layout is worth one look. The five floats after the vectors pack tightly, from offset 80
in steps of 4 — scalars align to 4, not to 16 — so the block is 100 bytes. The C++ side pads
it to 112 and pins every offset that matters:

```cpp title="code/src/2.lighting/light_casters/main.cpp"
static_assert(offsetof(Light, constant_term) == 80);
static_assert(offsetof(Light, outer_cutoff) == 96);
static_assert(sizeof(Light) == 112);
```

## Choosing the kind: a specialisation constant

Which kind of light the scene uses does not change while the program runs. That makes it a
perfect fit for the mechanism chapter 1.9 introduced and has not used since:

```slang title="code/src/2.lighting/light_casters/light_casters.slang"
[[vk::constant_id(0)]] const int kLightType = 2;

switch (kLightType) {
case kDirectional: ...
case kPoint: ...
default: ...
}
```

```cpp title="code/src/2.lighting/light_casters/main.cpp"
const ShaderConstants constants{};
const std::array<VkSpecializationMapEntry, 1> entries{{
    {.constantID = 0, .offset = offsetof(ShaderConstants, light_type),
     .size = sizeof(ShaderConstants::light_type)},
}};
```

The value is fixed when the pipeline is created, so the `switch` compiles to a SPIR-V
`OpSwitch` on a constant — and a driver is free to fold that away and keep only the case
that will run. A uniform would work too, and would leave a genuine branch in every fragment
of every frame for a value that never changes.

The specialisation is attached only to the lit pipeline. The lamp pipeline is built from the
same module and needs none: its fragment entry point never reads `kLightType`.

## What changed since 2.4

- `Light` gained `direction`, three attenuation terms and two cone cutoffs (112 bytes)
- the light's kind is a specialisation constant on the lit pipeline
- three crates became ten, standing still, at 1.14's positions
- the spotlight is rebuilt from the camera each frame; the lamp is drawn only for a point light

## Running it

```bash
./code/out/build/linux-debug/bin/2.5.light_casters
```

Sweep the torch across the scene and watch the soft edge move over the crates; walk toward a
crate and see it brighten as the attenuation falls away. To see the other two kinds, change
`kLightType` in `main.cpp` to `LightType::point` or `LightType::directional` and rebuild.

## Exercises

1. Make the cone's edge hard. Replace the `saturate(...)` line with
   `float cone = step(light.outer_cutoff, theta);` — since shaders are compiled when the sample
   starts, you can edit the copy in `bin/shaders/2.5.light_casters/` and just run again. The pool of light keeps its size and its falloff with distance, but its edge becomes a sharp line — clearest across the planks of the crate to the left of centre, where the soft gradient collapses to a single hard boundary. That is the difference one `saturate` makes.
2. Widen the torch to 25° inner and 35° outer, then narrow it to 5° and 7°. Notice that the
   narrow beam reaches just as far: the cone limits *where* light goes, attenuation limits *how
   far*.
3. Set the quadratic term to `0.0` and the linear term to `0.0` for the point light. The light
   no longer weakens at all, and the scene looks like it is lit by something much larger than a
   bulb. Then try quadratic `0.2`, and find the distance at which the light effectively ends.
4. Scale the ambient term by `intensity` too, and look at the crates outside the cone. Decide
   whether that is more realistic or less — and what it would take to do ambient properly
   (Part 6 answers that).
5. Replace the specialisation constant with a field in `Globals` and switch the light type at
   run time with a key press. It works. Now think about what the shader is doing on every
   fragment that it was not doing before.
