5.4's crates are cubes with a picture of a crate on each face. Under a lamp the picture gives
them away: light falls on a plank and on the gap beside it in exactly the same way, because as
far as the lighting knows, both are the same flat square. A rivet painted in light grey is lit
as flat as the steel around it.

Modelling the detail would fix that, at a price. A rivet that catches the light as a dome needs
a dome, and a dome that looks round up close needs a few hundred triangles. Each crate face
has eight rivets, four grooves and a bevelled frame, so the twelve triangles of a crate would
become more than ten thousand, and the shadow pass would draw every one of them eighteen times.

The lighting does not need any of that geometry. Every lighting calculation since 2.2 has used
the position for distances and for the directions to the lamp and to the eye; what decides how
a surface turns towards them is the **normal**. Much of what the eye reads as shape comes from
how brightness changes across a surface. Give each texel of a face its own normal, and the face is lit as if it had
the shape those normals describe, while staying two triangles.

## A direction per texel

A **normal map** is a texture whose texels are directions. A direction is three numbers
between −1 and 1, and an RGBA8 texel holds four numbers between 0 and 255, so each component c
is stored as the byte nearest to (c × 0.5 + 0.5) × 255. Reading it back is the reverse: the
shader is handed byte / 255, and doubles it and subtracts one.

The direction a normal map stores is not a world-space direction. The crate's map is drawn
once and used on all six faces of every crate, each facing a different way, and on crates
rotated by different angles. A world-space normal per texel would be right for one face of one
crate. What the map stores instead is the normal relative to the surface it is painted on:
how far it leans from straight out, and in which direction across the texture. The frame those
directions are measured in is the surface's **tangent space**:

- x points across the image, the direction in which u increases
- y points up the image
- z points straight out of the surface

A flat patch of surface has the normal (0, 0, 1) in this frame: no lean either way, and all of
it outwards. That stores as about (128, 128, 255), which is a pale violet blue. A normal map is
blue wherever the surface is flat, and every other colour in it is a lean. Red above 128 leans
towards +x, green above 128 leans up the image.

Up the image deserves care, because in Vulkan v runs the other way. Since 1.11 the first row
of an image has been v = 0 and v grows down the image, so "up the image" is the direction of
decreasing v. The choice of y as up is not this series' to make freely: a normal map is drawn
by one program and read by another, and the two have to agree. This series uses the same
convention as glTF, whose specification puts +X to the right, +Y up and +Z towards the viewer.
Many tools can also write the opposite, with y pointing down the image; key 3 shows what
reading such a map with the wrong assumption looks like. Which way y points is the map's
**normal map convention**, and it is not recorded anywhere in the file.

## Drawing the map

The two maps, `lvk_crate_normal.png` and `lvk_ground_normal.png`, are drawn by
`tools/make_textures.py` like every other texture in the series, and they are drawn the way
normal maps usually are: from a shape.

A **height field** is a surface given as a height above each point of a plane, z = h(x, y). The
crate's height field follows the same regions its diffuse map was painted from: the steel frame
stands at the top, the rivets rise from it as caps of a sphere, the planks sit lower with a
three-texel slope down from the frame's inner edge, and each gap between planks is a groove,
sloping to nothing over three texels on either side. The edges are slopes, not steps, because a
step has all of its slope in one texel, which becomes a line one texel wide in the normal map:
the narrowest feature a texture can hold, and the first to alias.

The normal of a height field follows from its two slopes. Move one unit along x and the surface
rises by ∂h/∂x, so the vector (1, 0, ∂h/∂x) lies in the surface; so does (0, 1, ∂h/∂y). Their
cross product is perpendicular to both:

```
(1, 0, ∂h/∂x) × (0, 1, ∂h/∂y) = (−∂h/∂x, −∂h/∂y, 1)
```

Normalised, that is the normal. The slopes come from a **central difference**: the height one
texel ahead minus the height one texel behind, over the two texels between them. It is centred
on the texel it is for, which a one-sided difference is not, and that keeps every feature where
the height field put it instead of half a texel to one side.

```python title="tools/make_textures.py"
            dh_dx = (h(x + 1.0, y) - h(x - 1.0, y)) / 2.0
            dh_dup = (h(x, y - 1.0) - h(x, y + 1.0)) / 2.0

            nx, ny, nz = normalise(-dh_dx, -dh_dup, 1.0)
```

`dh_dup` is the slope *up* the image, the height one row above minus one row below, because y
in the map points up and the rows count down. The function scales the heights before taking
differences; the scale sets how steep every edge is. In the crate's map the sides of the grooves
lean up to 57° from straight out and the sides of the rivets up to 55°; the frame's inner bevel
is the steepest edge, at up to 81°.

Reading back a few texels confirms the convention. A flat plank stores (128, 128, 255). The
rivet centred on texel (28, 28) stores (131, 171, 247) six texels above its centre, where its
surface faces up the image, and (132, 70, 241) six texels below. Six texels to its right it
stores (185, 123, 241), and six to its left (84, 124, 247). Up is green above 128 and right is
red above 128.

The 128 is worth a second look. Zero would be stored exactly by 127.5, which a byte cannot
hold, so the flat normal is stored as 128 and read back as 2 × 128 / 255 − 1 = 0.0039 instead of
0. A flat texel leans by 0.32°, which no one will see.

## A tangent at every corner

To use a direction measured in tangent space, the shader has to know, at each point, which
world direction each axis of that frame points in. z is easy: it is the normal the vertices
already carry. x and y are the surface directions in which the texture's u and v change, and
those depend on how the texture was laid onto the triangles. Nothing in 5.4's vertices says,
so this chapter works it out.

The **tangent** is the direction along the surface in which u increases. The **bitangent** is
the third axis, along the surface and perpendicular to the tangent; this series points it up
the image, towards decreasing v. Tangent, bitangent and normal together are the axes of tangent
space at a point.

### Solving for them

Across one triangle, position is a linear function of uv. Moving du along u and dv along v moves
the point by du ∂p/∂u + dv ∂p/∂v, where ∂p/∂u and ∂p/∂v are two unknown vectors, the same
everywhere on the triangle. Each edge of the triangle is a move whose uv difference is known.
For the edges from corner a to b and from a to c:

```
edge1 = du1 * ∂p/∂u + dv1 * ∂p/∂v
edge2 = du2 * ∂p/∂u + dv2 * ∂p/∂v
```

That is two vector equations for two vector unknowns, the same 2×2 system for each of x, y and
z. Its determinant is d = du1 dv2 − du2 dv1, and the solution is:

```
∂p/∂u = (edge1 * dv2 − edge2 * dv1) / d
∂p/∂v = (edge2 * du1 − edge1 * du2) / d
```

The determinant is twice the triangle's signed area in uv. When it is zero, the triangle's uvs
lie on one line, it covers no area of the texture, and there is no ∂p/∂u to find. No triangle in
this scene is like that, but a model from elsewhere can have them, so the code skips them.

The tables of corners stay as 5.4 wrote them, under a new name, and a function turns them into
the vertices the shader reads:

```cpp title="code/src/5.advanced_lighting/normal_mapping/main.cpp"
[[nodiscard]] std::vector<Vertex> with_tangents(std::span<const Corner> corners,
                                                std::span<const uint16_t> indices) {
    std::vector<glm::vec3> along_u(corners.size(), glm::vec3(0.0F));
    std::vector<glm::vec3> along_v(corners.size(), glm::vec3(0.0F));

    for (size_t i = 0; i + 2 < indices.size(); i += 3) {
        const std::array<uint16_t, 3> ids{indices[i], indices[i + 1], indices[i + 2]};
        const Corner& a = corners[ids[0]];
        const Corner& b = corners[ids[1]];
        const Corner& c = corners[ids[2]];

        const glm::vec3 edge1 = b.position - a.position;
        const glm::vec3 edge2 = c.position - a.position;
        const glm::vec2 duv1 = b.uv - a.uv;
        const glm::vec2 duv2 = c.uv - a.uv;

        // The determinant of the two uv differences. Zero means the triangle's uvs lie on
        // one line, so it covers no area of the texture and there is no dp_du to find.
        // None of this chapter's meshes has such a triangle; a model from elsewhere can.
        const float det = duv1.x * duv2.y - duv2.x * duv1.y;
        if (std::abs(det) < 1e-12F) {
            continue;
        }
        const float r = 1.0F / det;
        const glm::vec3 dp_du = (edge1 * duv2.y - edge2 * duv1.y) * r;
        const glm::vec3 dp_dv = (edge2 * duv1.x - edge1 * duv2.x) * r;

        for (const uint16_t id : ids) {
            along_u[id] += dp_du;
            along_v[id] += dp_dv;
        }
    }
```

Each corner sums ∂p/∂u and ∂p/∂v over every triangle that uses it. On a smooth mesh that is an
average across the corner's neighbours, the same idea 3.1 described for vertex normals. The
cube's corners each belong to one face, so each of its sums comes from one or both of that
face's two triangles, which agree.

### Straightening and handedness

A sum of ∂p/∂u vectors is along the surface in the average, but not exactly perpendicular to
the corner's normal, which was averaged separately. Taking away its component along the normal,
then normalising, leaves a unit vector that is perpendicular to the normal. That is
**Gram-Schmidt**: making a set of vectors perpendicular by removing from each its projection
onto the ones already fixed. Here there are only two, and one step does it.

```cpp title="code/src/5.advanced_lighting/normal_mapping/main.cpp"
    std::vector<Vertex> vertices;
    vertices.reserve(corners.size());
    for (size_t i = 0; i < corners.size(); ++i) {
        const Corner& corner = corners[i];
        const glm::vec3 n = corner.normal;

        glm::vec3 t = along_u[i] - n * glm::dot(n, along_u[i]);
        if (glm::length(t) < 1e-6F) {
            // No triangle gave this corner a direction. Any direction along the surface
            // will do, because nothing that reaches here is normal mapped.
            const glm::vec3 other =
                std::abs(n.x) < 0.9F ? glm::vec3(1, 0, 0) : glm::vec3(0, 1, 0);
            t = glm::cross(n, other);
        }
        t = glm::normalize(t);

        // The image's first row is v = 0, so up the image is towards decreasing v.
        const glm::vec3 up = -along_v[i];
        const float handedness = glm::dot(glm::cross(n, t), up) < 0.0F ? -1.0F : 1.0F;

        vertices.push_back(Vertex{
            .position = corner.position,
            .normal = n,
            .uv = corner.uv,
            .tangent = glm::vec4(t, handedness),
        });
    }
    return vertices;
}
```

The bitangent is not stored. Once the normal and the tangent are fixed, a unit vector
perpendicular to both can point only one of two ways: cross(n, t), or the opposite. Which one is
up the image depends on how the texture was laid down, and the fourth component of the
tangent records it as +1 or −1. That sign is the frame's **handedness**: whether x, y and z, in
that order, turn the way the right-hand rule from 1.14 says, or the mirror way.

It is −1 where the texture was laid on back to front. Modellers often do that on purpose: a
symmetric object, like the two halves of a face, can use one half of the texture for both sides
by mirroring the uvs of one side, which halves the texture. Those are **mirrored UVs**, and on
the mirrored side the image's up is the other perpendicular. Nothing in this scene is mirrored.
Logging every corner's tangent at start-up shows +1 on all six faces of the cube and on the
floor: the front face has its tangent along +x and cross(n, t) = +y, which is up both in the
world and in the image.

The lamp spheres go through the same function, and their poles are the one place it has
nothing to work with. A whole row of corners sits at each pole, at one point but with different
u, so a triangle with two of its corners there has an edge of zero length, and contributes
nothing to ∂p/∂u. Most pole corners also belong to a triangle that reaches the next ring and
get their direction from that. One corner in each pole row has no such triangle, the first at
the top and the last at the bottom: of the sphere's 561 corners, those 2 come out with no
direction and take an arbitrary one. The lamps are never
normal mapped, so nothing reads it.

```slang title="code/src/5.advanced_lighting/normal_mapping/scene.slang"
    output.normal = mul(draw.normal_matrix, vertex.normal);
    output.uv = vertex.uv;
    // The tangent lies along the surface, so it moves with the model matrix, as an edge
    // of a triangle would. w = 0 because it is a direction: no translation. The normal
    // matrix is only for the normal, which is perpendicular to the surface.
    output.tangent = float4(mul(draw.model, float4(vertex.tangent.xyz, 0.0)).xyz,
                            vertex.tangent.w);
```

The tangent and the normal go through different matrices, and the difference is the one 2.2
drew for the normal matrix. A tangent is a direction *within* the surface: the difference
between two nearby points on it. Whatever the model matrix does to the points, it does to their
difference, so the tangent transforms like an edge, by the model matrix, with w = 0 so that the
translation drops out. The normal is the one direction that has to stay perpendicular to all
such edges, and a non-uniform scale does not keep it so. That is why the normal goes through
the inverse transpose, and why the tangent must not: put the tangent through the normal matrix
and, under a non-uniform scale, it would leave the surface.

The handedness sign passes through untouched. A model matrix that mirrors the object, one with
a negative determinant as in 4.2, reverses which way every cross product of transformed vectors
points, so the bitangent would come out upside down unless w were multiplied by the
determinant's sign. Nothing in this scene is mirrored, and the shader does not do it.

## Reading the map

The fragment shader now has three things that vary across the triangle: the normal, the tangent
and the uv. Wherever the vertices disagree, interpolation leaves both vectors shorter than one,
and the tangent not quite perpendicular to the interpolated normal, because the two were
interpolated separately. On the cube's flat faces the vertices agree and nothing goes wrong, but
the shader cannot know that. Gram-Schmidt repairs both, exactly as on the CPU:

```slang title="code/src/5.advanced_lighting/normal_mapping/scene.slang"
float3 shading_normal(VSOutput input, float3 normal) {
    uint flags = push.globals.flags;
    if ((flags & kNormalMapsOn) == 0) {
        return normal;
    }

    // Interpolation leaves the tangent neither unit length nor quite perpendicular to
    // the interpolated normal. Removing its component along the normal, then
    // normalising, restores both: Gram-Schmidt, one step of it.
    float3 tangent = input.tangent.xyz;
    tangent = normalize(tangent - normal * dot(normal, tangent));
    // The third axis, up the image. w says whether up the image is this cross product or
    // its opposite; main.cpp worked out which, from the uvs.
    float3 bitangent = cross(normal, tangent) * input.tangent.w;

    // Each channel was stored as c * 0.5 + 0.5, to fit -1..1 into a UNORM's 0..1.
    float3 local = normal_map.Sample(input.uv).xyz * 2.0 - 1.0;
    if ((flags & kFlipGreen) != 0) {
        local.y = -local.y;
    }

    // The three columns of the basis, weighted by the three components: the matrix
    // whose columns are T, B and N, times the vector. Filtering and mipmapping average
    // texels, and an average of unit vectors is shorter than one, so it is normalised.
    return normalize(tangent * local.x + bitangent * local.y + normal * local.z);
}
```

The last line is a matrix product written out. The matrix whose columns are the tangent, the
bitangent and the normal, all in world space, is the **TBN matrix**. A direction with components
(x, y, z) in tangent space is x of the first axis, y of the second and z of the third, which is
the TBN matrix times (x, y, z), and that is what the line computes, a column at a time. 1.12
called this reading a matrix as its columns: the columns are where the frame's axes end up.

Its columns are perpendicular unit vectors, so its inverse is its transpose. That allows the
opposite arrangement, which is common: carry the lamp and eye directions *into* tangent space
with the transpose, once per vertex, and compare them with the map's normal as it stands. It
pays when one direction per lamp can be computed at the vertices and interpolated. Here each
fragment computes its own direction to each of three lamps anyway, and the shadow lookups need
world-space positions, so the shader carries the one normal out instead.

The normalisation at the end is not tidiness. Bilinear filtering averages four texels, and the
mip chain that `vkc::load_texture` builds averages every 2×2 block into one texel of the next
level. An average of unit vectors is shorter than one unit wherever they disagree: two normals
60° apart average to a vector of length cos 30° = 0.87. Unnormalised, every edge in the map would
come out dimmer than the flat surface beside it, and dimmer still at a distance, where smaller
mip levels average more.

The result of all this is the **shading normal**, the one the lighting uses. The interpolated
vertex normal, which describes the surface the triangles actually form, is the **geometric
normal**. On these flat faces it is exactly the triangle's own normal. Until this chapter the two
were the same thing. The fragment shader now keeps both:

```slang title="code/src/5.advanced_lighting/normal_mapping/scene.slang"
[shader("fragment")]
float4 fragmentMain(VSOutput input) : SV_Target {
    float3 surface = normalize(input.normal);
    float3 normal = shading_normal(input, surface);

    // A direction drawn as a colour: each component from -1..1 to 0..1. Facing +x is
    // red, +y green, +z blue.
    if ((push.globals.flags & kShowNormals) != 0) {
        return float4(normal * 0.5 + 0.5, 1.0);
    }

    float3 tint = push.draws[push.draw_index].colour.rgb;
    float3 albedo = base_colour_map.Sample(input.uv).rgb * tint;
    return float4(shade(input, surface, normal, albedo), 1.0);
}
```

and passes both to `shade`, which passes both to each lamp:

```slang title="code/src/5.advanced_lighting/normal_mapping/scene.slang"
float3 shade(VSOutput input, float3 surface, float3 normal, float3 albedo) {
    Globals globals = *push.globals;

    float3 to_eye = normalize(globals.view_position.xyz - input.world_position);

    float3 total = globals.ambient.rgb * albedo;
    for (uint i = 0; i < kLampCount; ++i) {
        total += lamp_light(globals.lamps[i], input.world_position, normal, surface,
                            to_eye, albedo) *
                 lamp_visibility(i, input.world_position);
    }
    return total;
}
```

### Two normals, two questions

`lamp_light` asks two questions of the surface, and they want different normals:

```slang title="code/src/5.advanced_lighting/normal_mapping/scene.slang"
float3 lamp_light(Lamp lamp, float3 world_position, float3 normal, float3 surface,
                  float3 to_eye, float3 albedo) {
    float3 offset = lamp.position.xyz - world_position;
    float distance = length(offset);
    float3 to_light = offset / distance;

    float attenuation = 1.0 / (kConstantTerm + kLinearTerm * distance +
                               kQuadraticTerm * distance * distance);

    if (dot(surface, to_light) <= 0.0) {
        return float3(0.0);
    }
    float facing = max(dot(normal, to_light), 0.0);
```

The first is 5.1's facing test: is the lamp in front of this surface at all? That is a question
about the surface the triangles form, and it uses the geometric normal. A normal map can lean the shading normal
by 60° or more, so a lamp a little behind a face can be in front of a texel's shading normal,
and without the test, light would reach the side of a face turned away from it. The second
question is how much light the surface takes in and reflects towards the eye, and that is the
shading normal's job: Lambert's cosine and the specular lobe both use it.

The opening view has such a place. Move the facing test onto the shading normal and 1,874 pixels
change, by up to 109 levels out of 255, most of them in one strip: the right-hand edge of
the big crate's front face, where the frame's outer bevel leans round the corner towards the
lamp that lights the side face. The front face itself is turned away from that lamp, and the
shadow does not remove the strip: these counts are with shadows on.

Which picture is right is not clear-cut, and that is the honest summary of the guard. A real
bevel on a real corner would catch that lamp, so the strip on the right is what the shape the map
describes would do. But it is light arriving at a surface the lamp is behind, and the rule that
lets it through has no limit: any texel whose map leans towards a lamp is lit by it, on whatever
face. The guard gives up the strip to keep lamps off faces they are behind. With shadows switched
off as well, the two tests differ on 4,365 pixels, spread across more of the crates' faces.

## UNORM, not SRGB

A normal map is data, like 2.4's specular map, and is loaded with a UNORM format so that the
shader receives byte / 255 untouched. Key 2 loads the same file a second time with
`VK_FORMAT_R8G8B8A8_SRGB` and binds that instead:

```cpp title="code/src/5.advanced_lighting/normal_mapping/main.cpp"
        // A normal map is data, like 2.4's specular map: its bytes are directions, and
        // the only right format is UNORM, which hands the shader byte / 255 unchanged.
        // The SRGB copy is key 2's, and is there only to be wrong.
        const auto load_pair = [&](const char* file, std::array<vkc::Image, 2>& pair) {
            const std::filesystem::path path = vkc::asset_path(file);
            pair[static_cast<uint32_t>(NormalRead::Unorm)] =
                vkc::load_texture(context(), path, VK_FORMAT_R8G8B8A8_UNORM);
            pair[static_cast<uint32_t>(NormalRead::Srgb)] =
                vkc::load_texture(context(), path, VK_FORMAT_R8G8B8A8_SRGB);
        };
        load_pair("textures/lvk_crate_normal.png", crate_normals_);
        load_pair("textures/lvk_ground_normal.png", floor_normals_);
```

```cpp title="code/src/5.advanced_lighting/normal_mapping/main.cpp"
// The pair of normal maps a surface has: the right way to read one, and key 2's wrong
// way. The two images hold the same bytes; only the format they were created with
// differs.
enum class NormalRead : uint32_t { Unorm = 0, Srgb = 1 };
constexpr std::array<const char*, 2> kReadNames{"read as UNORM", "read as SRGB"};
```

Through the SRGB image the sampler applies 2.4's decoding curve to every channel before the
shader sees it. The curve leaves 0 and 1 where they are and pulls everything between them
down. A flat texel's 128 becomes 0.216 instead of 0.502, which the shader turns into −0.568
instead of 0. Its 255 stays 1. The flat normal (0, 0, 1) is read as (−0.568, −0.568, 1), which
normalises to a direction 38.8° from straight out, leaning towards −x and −y: left and down the
image. Every flat texel on every surface leans that way, and the features lean with it.

<figure>
  <img src="/learn_vulkan/img/5.5/srgb.png" alt="The same crate face twice, side by side. Left: correctly lit, with a bright highlight across the planks on the left. Right: the whole face is darker and evenly orange, the highlight is gone, and the grooves are darker lines with little relief" />
  <figcaption>Left: the normal map read as UNORM. Right: as SRGB, key 2. Every flat texel leans 38.8°
  left and down, away from the lamp.</figcaption>
</figure>

It is easy to make this mistake, because a normal map is a PNG like a colour texture and
`vkc::load_texture` defaults to SRGB. Nothing reports it. The validation layers cannot know what
the bytes mean, and the scene still looks lit. It looks lit from the wrong place.

## Which way is up

Key 3 negates the green component after it is read, which is what reading a map drawn in the
other convention, with y pointing down the image, would do. Red is untouched, so nothing changes
side to side. Everything that leans up the image now leans down it.

A flipped map looks wrong in a particular way: lit from the side as it should be, but lit from
below when the light is above. Only the vertical lean is reversed, so the fault is easiest to see
on round features like the rivets, whose highlight slides to the wrong half. When a map from
elsewhere does this, the cure is to negate green, either once in the image or every time in the
shader, as key 3 does.

## What a normal map cannot do

Look at the edges of the crates. Every outline is still a straight line: the frame's bevel, so
convincing on the face, does not round the crate's corner, and a rivet near the edge of a face
does not break its outline. The outline of an object against what is behind it, its **silhouette**,
is drawn by the triangles, and a normal map does not move a single one.

The shadows say the same thing. The shadow pass reads only positions; the rivets cast no
shadows and the grooves catch none, and each crate's shadow is the shadow of a cube. A normal map
changes how light is reflected, not where the surface is, so anything that depends on where the
surface is, its outline, what it hides, what it shadows, is untouched. That limit is what the
next chapter starts on.

## What changed since 5.4

- a normal map each for the crates and the floor, drawn from height fields by
  `tools/make_textures.py`
- `Corner` for the hand-written tables, and a 48-byte `Vertex` with a tangent and its handedness
- `with_tangents`, solving each triangle for ∂p/∂u and ∂p/∂v
- the tangent carried into world space by the model matrix, and the TBN basis built per fragment
- separate geometric and shading normals: the facing test on one, the lighting on the other
- set 0's second binding, and an SRGB copy of each map for key 2
- 5.4's comparison keys removed: only the multiview fill and the 3×3 reads remain
- 39 chapters in the build now

## Words introduced in this chapter

| | |
|---|---|
| **normal map** | a texture whose texels are directions, each stored as c × 0.5 + 0.5, giving the surface a normal per texel instead of per vertex |
| **height field** | a surface given as a height above each point of a plane, z = h(x, y) |
| **central difference** | a slope estimated from the values one step ahead and one step behind, centred on the point it is for |
| **tangent space** | the frame a normal map's directions are measured in: x across the image, y up it, z out of the surface |
| **tangent** | the direction along a surface in which the texture's u increases |
| **bitangent** | the third axis of tangent space, along the surface and perpendicular to the tangent; here, up the image |
| **handedness** | whether a frame's three axes turn the way the right-hand rule says or the mirror way; stored as ±1 in the tangent's w |
| **mirrored UVs** | uvs laid onto part of a model back to front, so that one half of a texture serves both halves of a symmetric object; there the handedness is −1 |
| **TBN matrix** | the matrix whose columns are the tangent, the bitangent and the normal, which carries a tangent-space direction into world space |
| **Gram-Schmidt** | making vectors perpendicular by removing from each its projection onto the ones already fixed |
| **shading normal** | the normal the lighting uses: here, the normal map's direction carried into the world |
| **geometric normal** | the normal the vertices give, describing the surface the triangles actually form; it says which side of that surface a lamp is on |
| **normal map convention** | which way a normal map's y axis points, up the image or down it; nothing in the file records it |
| **silhouette** | an object's outline against what is behind it, drawn by its triangles and untouched by a normal map |
