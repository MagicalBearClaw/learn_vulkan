Part 2 lit its crates with the **Phong reflection model**, and the specular term in 2.2 was
one line: reflect the light about the normal, and raise the cosine of the angle between that
reflection and the eye to the shininess. Part 2's review listed that line among the things
later chapters would change, because it has a flaw that the crates never showed. It shows as
soon as a light sits close to a large surface and the eye looks along it: the highlight ends
in a hard curve, and nothing beyond that curve gets any specular light at all, however dull
the surface is meant to be.

This chapter works out where that curve is and why it exists, and then replaces the angle
Phong measures with a different one. The replacement is **Blinn-Phong**, which Jim Blinn
proposed in 1977. It is the specular term this series uses from now on, until Part 6
replaces the whole model. It is a one-line change to the shader, and the rest of the chapter
is what that line does to the picture: the exponent has to change with it, the highlight
changes shape, and a flaw both models share becomes much more visible.

The sample is 4.5's room with the mirror, the sky and the ferns taken out, at night, with
one lamp hanging just above the floor. The keys:

- <kbd>1</kbd> switch between Phong and Blinn-Phong (Blinn-Phong is the default)
- <kbd>2</kbd> cycle the shininess through 1, 4, 16, 64 and 256 (4 is the default)
- <kbd>3</kbd> match the exponents: give Blinn-Phong four times the shininess, or the same
  (matched is the default)

Every setting prints what the shader is now using:

```
[info] 1 Phong / Blinn-Phong, 2 shininess, 3 match exponents.
[info] Blinn-Phong, shininess 16 (4 x 4)
```

## Where Phong's highlight ends

Phong's specular term is `pow(max(dot(to_eye, reflected), 0.0), shininess)`. The `max` is
there because a cosine goes negative once the angle passes 90°, and a negative number raised
to a fractional power is not a number. So wherever the eye is more than 90° away from the
reflected light, Phong's specular is zero — not small, zero — and it is zero at every
shininess, because the test happens before the exponent.

On a small object seen from in front of the light that never matters: the reflected ray
points back roughly toward the eye everywhere the eye can see. On a floor it matters a great
deal, and the place it stops being true can be worked out exactly.

Put the floor at height 0, the lamp at height *h* above a point **L** on the floor, and the
eye at height *c* above a point **C**. For a point **P** on the floor, the light arrives
travelling from the lamp down to **P**. A floor's normal is straight up, and reflecting about
it keeps the horizontal part of a direction and flips the vertical part, so the reflected ray
leaves **P** heading horizontally away from the lamp, along **P** − **L**, and upward by *h*:

```
reflected  ∝  (P − L,  h)       horizontal part, then height
to_eye     ∝  (C − P,  c)
```

Neither is unit length, but only the sign of their dot product matters, and the lengths are
positive. The dot product is zero where

```
(P − L) · (C − P) + h c  =  0
```

and a little algebra turns that into a familiar shape. Write **M** for the midpoint of **L**
and **C**, and *d* for the distance between them. Expanding, and completing the square:

```
|P − M|²  =  d²/4 + h c
```

That is a **circle** on the floor, centred halfway between the lamp's foot and the eye's
foot, with a radius a little more than half the distance between them. Inside it Phong has a
highlight. Outside it the eye is more than 90° from every reflected ray, and there is none.

In the sample *h* is 0.55, *c* is 1.35 and *d* is 7.0, which puts the far edge of the circle
√(12.25 + 0.7425) − 3.5 = 0.105 units beyond the lamp's foot. Projected through the
camera, that is row 386.7 of the 720-row frame, straight below the lamp. Rendering only the
specular term, with the shininess at 1, and reading the pixels in that column:

| Row | 330 | … | 381 | 384 | 387 | 390 | 393 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Phong | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 49 | 105 | 133 |
| Blinn-Phong | 53 | … | 140 | 150 | 158 | 166 | 171 |

Zero at row 384 and 49 at row 387. The circle is where the arithmetic said.

Strictly, Phong's highlight does not jump. The cosine reaches zero continuously at the
circle, and `max` then holds it there. What the eye sees is a **crease**: brightness falling
steadily and then stopping, with a sudden change of slope. At shininess 1 the fall is a
straight line in the cosine, and the swapchain's sRGB encoding, which 1.4 chose, stretches
the darkest values the most — in the table, 0 to 49 to 105 in six rows of pixels — so the
crease reads as an edge. At higher shininess the cosine has fallen to almost nothing well
before the circle and there is nothing left to cut, which is why 2.2's crates, at 32,
never showed it.

The general name for what Phong is doing is **lobe clipping**. The **specular lobe** is the
shape of the specular term around its peak, over every direction the eye could be in. A
rough surface has a wide lobe, and Phong centres its lobe on the reflected ray and throws away
whatever half of it lies more than 90° away. Near a **grazing angle** — light or eye nearly
along the surface — the reflected ray itself is nearly along the surface, so part of what
gets thrown away lies above the surface, where the eye can be. Real rough floors are not
dark behind a lamp.

## The halfway vector

Blinn's idea is to measure a different angle. Ask what orientation a tiny mirror at **P**
would need in order to reflect the light straight into the eye. A mirror reflects when its
normal bisects the incoming and outgoing directions, so that normal is the **halfway
vector**:

```
H  =  normalize(to_light + to_eye)
```

Blinn-Phong's cosine is how closely the surface's actual normal lines up with it:
`dot(normal, H)`. When the surface is exactly the right mirror, *N* equals *H* and the
cosine is 1, which is exactly where Phong's cosine is 1 as well, since the reflected ray then
points straight at the eye. Away from that peak the two measure different things, and the
difference is the whole chapter.

The first difference is that Blinn-Phong's cosine is never negative where it matters. Call
the half of all directions on the surface's outward side its **hemisphere**. A point the eye
can see has the eye in that hemisphere, so `dot(N, to_eye)` ≥ 0; a point the light reaches
has the light in it, so `dot(N, to_light)` ≥ 0. Then

```
dot(N, H)  =  (dot(N, to_light) + dot(N, to_eye)) / |to_light + to_eye|  ≥  0
```

because the top is a sum of two numbers that are not negative and the bottom is a length.
The bisector of two directions in a hemisphere is in the hemisphere. There is nothing for
`max` to clip, and no circle.

The half vector also has a history that explains why it was the default for a long time. If
the light is treated as infinitely far away, so that `to_light` is the same at every point,
and the eye is treated the same way — an **infinite viewer** — then *H* is the same at every
point too, and can be worked out once per light instead of once per pixel. OpenGL's
fixed-function lighting assumed an infinite viewer unless a program asked for a local one. This
sample has a lamp in the room and an eye in the room, so *H* changes at every pixel and
Blinn-Phong costs a `normalize` where Phong costs a `reflect`. Neither is the expensive part
of this shader.

## Four times the exponent

Switch from Phong to Blinn-Phong at the same shininess and the highlight changes size. The
reason is an angle that halves.

Take the case where the light, the eye and the normal all lie in one plane — the **plane of
incidence**. Measure angles in that plane from the normal: the light at −α, so its
reflection is at +α, and the eye at +β. The angle between the eye and the reflection is
|β − α|. *H* bisects −α and +β, so it sits at (β − α)/2, and the angle between *H* and the
normal is |β − α|/2. Exactly half.

So at the same exponent, Blinn-Phong's highlight falls off half as fast with angle, and is
twice as wide. To get the old width back, the exponent has to grow, and the factor comes
from how `cos` behaves near zero. The **small-angle approximation** says cos *x* ≈ 1 − *x*²/2,
and raising that to a large power *n* gives

```
cosⁿ(x)  ≈  (1 − x²/2)ⁿ  ≈  exp(−n x² / 2)
```

a bell curve whose width goes as 1/√*n*. Phong at exponent *n* with angle *x* is
exp(−*n x*²/2). Blinn-Phong at exponent *m* with angle *x*/2 is exp(−*m x*²/8). They are the
same curve when *m* = 4*n*. That is **exponent matching**, and it is what key <kbd>3</kbd>
does:

```cpp title="code/src/5.advanced_lighting/blinn_phong/main.cpp"
// The halfway vector sits at half the angle the reflected vector does, so the same
// highlight needs four times the exponent. The article derives the four.
constexpr float kBlinnExponentScale = 4.0F;
```

The same measurement as before, specular only, at Phong's shininess 16 and Blinn-Phong's
64, finding the rows straight below the lamp where the highlight is at least half its peak
(in linear light, before the sRGB encoding):

| | Rows at half the peak or brighter | Columns at the peak row |
|---|---|---|
| Phong, 16 | 414 to 551 | 576 to 703 — 128 wide |
| Blinn-Phong, 16 | 401 to 635 | 609 to 670 — 62 wide |
| Blinn-Phong, 64 | 413 to 552 | 622 to 657 — 36 wide |

Down the column, which is the plane of incidence, the matched pair agree to within a row.
Across it they do not: matched Blinn-Phong is less than a third of Phong's width.

### The streak

Out of the plane of incidence the angle does not simply halve. Put the normal straight up,
the light at angle θ from it on one side, and the eye at the same angle θ on the other side
but turned sideways by a small angle φ around the normal. Working through the dot products
with the small-angle approximation:

```
Phong:        dot(to_eye, reflected)  ≈  cos(φ sin θ)
Blinn-Phong:  dot(N, H)               ≈  cos(φ tan θ / 2)
```

With the exponents matched, the widths across the plane differ by the ratio of those two
angles:

```
(tan θ / 2) · 2  /  sin θ   =   1 / cos θ
```

At a normal viewing angle cos θ is close to 1 and the two agree. At a grazing angle cos θ is
small, and Blinn-Phong's highlight is thinner across than along by that factor: a streak
instead of a spot. In the sample the highlight's peak is where the floor reflects the lamp
into the eye, 2.03 units beyond the lamp's foot, where tan θ = 2.03 / 0.55 and θ is 74.8°.
The prediction is 1 / cos 74.8° = 3.8 times narrower. The table's 128 and 36 are a ratio of
3.6, with perspective, attenuation and the half-peak threshold all leaning on it.

  <figcaption>Specular only. Left, Phong at 16. Middle, Blinn-Phong at 16: twice as long,

This **highlight elongation** is not a flaw to be matched away. It is what glossy surfaces
do: a low sun on wet tarmac or on water makes a long streak toward the viewer, not a round
spot, because what reflects it is a scatter of small tilted facets, and at grazing angles a
small sideways tilt of a facet moves the reflection a long way. Phong's lobe is round about
the reflected ray whatever the angle, and so its highlight stays round. Part 6 builds the
facet argument properly; Blinn-Phong is its first approximation.

## Light on the wrong side

One test is still missing from both models. Neither cosine knows whether the light is in
front of the surface at all.

For Phong, the reflection keeps the light's angle to the normal: `dot(N, reflected)` equals
`dot(N, to_light)`, so if the light is behind the surface, the reflected ray points into it
by the same amount. The eye is in front of the surface, so it can only be within 90° of that
ray when both are close to grazing. Phong's leak is thin.

Blinn-Phong's is not. The formula for `dot(N, H)` above is still a sum over a length, and now
one term is negative. It is positive whenever the eye is further above the surface than the
light is below it — which, for a face turned away from the lamp and towards the camera, is
most of the time.

Taking the test out and measuring the difference over the frame:

| | Pixels that change | Largest change (of 255) |
|---|---|---|
| Phong, shininess 1 | 3,570 | 105 |
| Phong, shininess 4 | 1,373 | 29 |
| Blinn-Phong, 4 (4 × 1) | 67,728 | 71 |
| Blinn-Phong, 16 (4 × 4) | 41,553 | 4 |

Phong's changes are on the tops of the two nearest crates, and a sliver down the side of one.
The lamp is below the tops, so they face away from it, and the eye skims across them — both near grazing, as the argument predicted.
Blinn-Phong at a low exponent lights whole faces: the two crates nearest the camera have their
lamp side at the back, and without the test their fronts glow.

<figure>
  <img src="/learn_vulkan/img/5.1/specular-leak.png" alt="Two renders of the room. Left: the two nearest crates are dark on the side facing the camera. Right: the same two crate faces are a flat, dull grey, as if lit from the front" />
  <figcaption>Blinn-Phong at 4. Left, with the facing test. Right, without it: the faces
  turned away from the lamp pick up a highlight they cannot have.</figcaption>
</figure>

The **specular leak** is fixed by the **facing test** the diffuse term already has: if
`dot(N, to_light)` is not positive, the surface is facing away from the light and gets no
highlight. At higher exponents the leak shrinks to almost nothing — 4 levels out of 255 at
the default — which is why it is easy to ship without noticing, and why the test is in the
shader regardless.

## The shader

The structs match main.cpp's. `Globals` has the two new light fields and the two scalars:

```slang title="code/src/5.advanced_lighting/blinn_phong/scene.slang"
// Matches Globals in main.cpp, 200 bytes, scalar layout.
struct Globals {
    float4x4 view;
    float4x4 projection;
    float4 view_position;
    float4 light_position;
    float4 light_colour;
    float4 ambient;
    float shininess;
    uint blinn;
};
```

The specular strength and 2.5's attenuation constants:

```slang title="code/src/5.advanced_lighting/blinn_phong/scene.slang"
static const float kSpecularStrength = 0.5;

// 2.5's attenuation constants, unchanged. Chapter 5.7 replaces them with the
// inverse-square law.
static const float kConstantTerm = 1.0;
static const float kLinearTerm = 0.09;
static const float kQuadraticTerm = 0.032;
```

The vertex shader is 4.5's. The two cosines are one function each, so the choice between them
is visible as a choice:

```slang title="code/src/5.advanced_lighting/blinn_phong/scene.slang"
// Phong's cosine: how closely the eye lines up with the light's mirror reflection.
float phong_cosine(float3 normal, float3 to_light, float3 to_eye) {
    float3 reflected = reflect(-to_light, normal);
    return dot(to_eye, reflected);
}

// Blinn's cosine: how closely the normal lines up with the direction halfway between the
// light and the eye -- the normal a mirror would need to send one into the other.
float blinn_cosine(float3 normal, float3 to_light, float3 to_eye) {
    float3 halfway = normalize(to_light + to_eye);
    return dot(normal, halfway);
}
```

`reflect(-to_light, normal)` is 2.2's reflection, of the direction the light travels, about
the normal. `normalize(to_light + to_eye)` is the halfway vector. Both vectors point away from
the surface, and both are unit length, so their sum points along the bisector.

Then `shade`, which is 2.5's point light with the specular term chosen by `globals.blinn`:

```slang title="code/src/5.advanced_lighting/blinn_phong/scene.slang"
float3 shade(VSOutput input, float3 albedo) {
    Globals globals = *push.globals;

    float3 normal = normalize(input.normal);
    float3 to_eye = normalize(globals.view_position.xyz - input.world_position);
    float3 offset = globals.light_position.xyz - input.world_position;
    float distance = length(offset);
    float3 to_light = offset / distance;

    float attenuation = 1.0 / (kConstantTerm + kLinearTerm * distance +
                               kQuadraticTerm * distance * distance);

    float facing = dot(normal, to_light);
    float diffuse_amount = max(facing, 0.0);

    float cosine = globals.blinn != 0 ? blinn_cosine(normal, to_light, to_eye)
                                      : phong_cosine(normal, to_light, to_eye);
    float specular_amount = pow(max(cosine, 0.0), globals.shininess);
    // A surface facing away from the light gets no highlight. Without this test Phong
    // leaks slivers at grazing angles and Blinn-Phong lights whole faces; the article
    // measures both.
    if (facing <= 0.0) {
        specular_amount = 0.0;
    }

    float3 diffuse = diffuse_amount * albedo;
    float3 specular = specular_amount * kSpecularStrength;
    float3 lit = globals.light_colour.rgb * (diffuse + specular) * attenuation;
    return globals.ambient.rgb * albedo + lit;
}
```

`*push.globals` reads the whole 200-byte struct through the pointer once, into a local
variable, instead of writing `push.globals.` in front of every field. The generated code is
the same shape either way. It is shorter to read.

`facing` is computed once and used twice: clamped for the diffuse term, and as the facing
test for the specular one. The branch on `globals.blinn` takes the same side for every pixel
of a frame, because every pixel reads the same globals.

The ambient is added once, outside the attenuation. It stands for light that has bounced
around the whole room, which does not fall off with distance from the lamp.

The fragment entry point is 4.5's without the cutout variant, and the lamp gets one of its
own that ignores lighting altogether:

```slang title="code/src/5.advanced_lighting/blinn_phong/scene.slang"
[shader("fragment")]
float4 fragmentMain(VSOutput input) : SV_Target {
    float3 tint = push.draws[push.draw_index].colour.rgb;
    float3 albedo = base_colour_map.Sample(input.uv).rgb * tint;
    return float4(shade(input, albedo), 1.0);
}

// The lamp itself: its colour, unlit.
[shader("fragment")]
float4 lampFragmentMain(VSOutput input) : SV_Target {
    return float4(push.draws[push.draw_index].colour.rgb, 1.0);
}
```

The three keys each flip or step one piece of state and print the result:

```cpp title="code/src/5.advanced_lighting/blinn_phong/main.cpp"
        camera_.handle_event(event, window().handle());

        if (event.type != SDL_EVENT_KEY_DOWN || event.key.repeat) {

                log_specular();
                break;

                break;
        }
    }
```

```cpp title="code/src/5.advanced_lighting/blinn_phong/main.cpp"
    void on_shutdown() override {
        const VkDevice device = context().device();

        vkDestroyPipeline(device, lamp_pipeline_, nullptr);
        vkDestroyPipeline(device, opaque_pipeline_, nullptr);
        vkDestroyPipelineLayout(device, pipeline_layout_, nullptr);
        vkDestroyDescriptorPool(device, descriptor_pool_, nullptr);
        vkDestroyDescriptorSetLayout(device, texture_set_layout_, nullptr);

        sampler_.destroy();
        floor_map_.destroy();
        crate_map_.destroy();

        for (FrameArena& arena : arenas_) {
            arena.destroy();
        }
        depth_.destroy();
        lamp_.destroy();
        floor_.destroy();
        cube_.destroy();
    }
```

There is one pass now, so `on_render` records it inline: the floor, the crates, then the lamp
with its own pipeline. The clear colour is black, because it is night:

```cpp title="code/src/5.advanced_lighting/blinn_phong/main.cpp"
    void on_render(const vkc::FrameInfo& frame) override {
        camera_.update(delta_time());

        // Everything the shaders will read this frame is written here, into this frame
        // slot's arena, in the order the frame happens to want it. The arena is emptied
        // first: the GPU finished with the last frame that used this slot before the
        // command buffer was handed over, so there is nothing left in it to protect.
        FrameArena& arena = arenas_[frame.frame_number % vkc::kFramesInFlight];
        arena.reset();

        write_globals(arena, frame.extent);
        write_draws(arena);

        vkc::begin_rendering(frame.cmd, frame.view, depth_.view(), frame.extent,
                             {{0.0F, 0.0F, 0.0F, 1.0F}});
        set_viewport(frame.cmd, frame.extent);

        bind_indices(frame.cmd, floor_);
        vkCmdBindPipeline(frame.cmd, VK_PIPELINE_BIND_POINT_GRAPHICS, opaque_pipeline_);
        bind_texture(frame.cmd, floor_set_);
        push(frame.cmd, floor_.vertex_address, kFloorDraw);
        vkCmdDrawIndexed(frame.cmd, floor_.index_count, 1, 0, 0, 0);

        bind_indices(frame.cmd, cube_);
        bind_texture(frame.cmd, crate_set_);
        for (uint32_t i = 0; i < kCrates.size(); ++i) {
            push(frame.cmd, cube_.vertex_address, kFirstCrateDraw + i);
            vkCmdDrawIndexed(frame.cmd, cube_.index_count, 1, 0, 0, 0);
        }

        // The lamp is drawn as its own colour and nothing else, so it reads as the
        // source of the light rather than as something the light falls on.
        bind_indices(frame.cmd, lamp_);
        vkCmdBindPipeline(frame.cmd, VK_PIPELINE_BIND_POINT_GRAPHICS, lamp_pipeline_);
        push(frame.cmd, lamp_.vertex_address, kLampDraw);
        vkCmdDrawIndexed(frame.cmd, lamp_.index_count, 1, 0, 0, 0);

        vkCmdEndRendering(frame.cmd);
    }
```

`exponent` is where matching happens. The shader does not know the exponent was scaled; it
raises whichever cosine it computed to whatever number it is given:

```cpp title="code/src/5.advanced_lighting/blinn_phong/main.cpp"
    // The exponent the shader is given. Matching is a CPU-side decision: the shader
    // raises whichever cosine it computed to whatever number arrives here, and does not
    // know or care that the number was scaled.
    [[nodiscard]] float exponent() const {
        const float shininess = kShininessSteps[shininess_step_];
        return blinn_ && matched_ ? shininess * kBlinnExponentScale : shininess;
    }

    void write_globals(FrameArena& arena, VkExtent2D extent) {
        const Globals globals{
            .view = camera_.view(),
            .projection = projection(extent),
            .view_position = glm::vec4(camera_.position, 1.0F),
            .light_position = glm::vec4(kLampPosition, 1.0F),
            .light_colour = glm::vec4(kLampColour, 1.0F),
            .ambient = glm::vec4(kAmbient, 1.0F),
            .shininess = exponent(),
            .blinn = blinn_ ? 1U : 0U,
        };
        globals_address_ = arena.write(&globals, sizeof(globals));
    }

    void write_draws(FrameArena& arena) {
        draws_.clear();

        draws_.push_back(draw_data_of(glm::mat4(1.0F), kWhite));

        for (const Placement& crate : kCrates) {
            draws_.push_back(draw_data_of(
                glm::translate(model_of(crate), glm::vec3(0.0F, 0.5F, 0.0F)), kWhite));
        }

        draws_.push_back(draw_data_of(glm::translate(glm::mat4(1.0F), kLampPosition),
                                      glm::vec4(kLampColour, 1.0F)));

        draws_address_ = arena.write(draws_.data(), draws_.size() * sizeof(DrawData));
    }
```

```cpp title="code/src/5.advanced_lighting/blinn_phong/main.cpp"
    [[nodiscard]] static glm::mat4 projection(VkExtent2D extent) {
        const float aspect =
            static_cast<float>(extent.width) / static_cast<float>(extent.height);
        glm::mat4 projection =
            glm::perspective(glm::radians(45.0F), aspect, 0.1F, 100.0F);
        projection[1][1] *= -1.0F;  // Vulkan's clip-space y points down.
        return projection;
    }
```

## What changed since 4.5

- a new part, and a new scene: 4.5's room at night, one lamp, no sky, no mirror, no ferns
- a point light with 2.5's attenuation, in place of the sun
- Blinn-Phong's halfway vector beside Phong's reflection vector, chosen per frame
- the exponent scaled by four when switching, and why four
- the facing test on the specular term
- one pass instead of seven, so one set of globals and a simpler `push`
- 35 chapters in the build now

## Words introduced in this chapter

| | |
|---|---|
| **Blinn-Phong** | Phong's model with the specular cosine measured between the normal and the halfway vector |
| **halfway vector** | `normalize(to_light + to_eye)`: the normal a mirror would need to reflect the light into the eye |
| **specular lobe** | the shape of the specular term around its peak, over all the directions the eye could be in |
| **grazing angle** | a direction nearly along a surface, far from its normal |
| **hemisphere** | the half of all directions on a surface's outward side |
| **lobe clipping** | Phong's `max` discarding the part of its lobe more than 90° from the reflected ray |
| **crease** | a sudden change in how fast brightness changes; the eye reads it as an edge |
| **plane of incidence** | the plane containing the normal and the incoming light |
| **exponent matching** | giving Blinn-Phong four times Phong's exponent, so the highlights agree in the plane of incidence |
| **small-angle approximation** | cos *x* ≈ 1 − *x*²/2 for small *x*; with it, cosⁿ*x* ≈ exp(−*n x*²/2) |
| **highlight elongation** | Blinn-Phong's highlight stretching into a streak at grazing angles, by 1 / cos θ |
| **specular leak** | a highlight on a surface facing away from the light |
| **facing test** | giving no specular where `dot(N, to_light)` is not positive |
| **infinite viewer** | treating the eye as infinitely far away, so `to_eye` and, with a distant light, *H* are constant |

## Exercises

1. Set the shininess to 1 with Phong, then fly the camera upward. Using the circle formula,
   predict what happens to the edge beyond the lamp as *c* grows, and check it.
2. Replace Phong's `max(cosine, 0.0)` with `abs(cosine)`. What does the floor do beyond the
   circle, and why is that worse than the clipping?
3. Turn matching off and switch between the models at shininess 256. Is the difference in
   the size of the highlight what the plane-of-incidence argument predicts?
4. Move the lamp up to 3 units above the floor. The ratio 1 / cos θ at the peak falls: work
   out its new value and see whether the streak really gets rounder.
5. Take the facing test out and find the lowest shininess at which you can no longer see the
   leak on the crates. Compare it with the table.
6. Make the light directional again — 4.5's sun — and compute *H* once on the CPU with the
   camera's forward direction standing in for `to_eye`. What does the infinite viewer get
   wrong on the floor?
