4.1 took apart what happens to a fragment's *depth*. This chapter takes apart what happens
to its *colour* — and, before that, the one piece of fixed-function state that runs before
the fragment shader rather than after it.

The scene has three kinds of surface in it. The crates and the floor are opaque. The ferns
look like plants and are flat quads with most of their texture thrown away. The panes are
transparent, and they are the only things here that had to be sorted by hand.

Four keys switch between the variants, so every failure this chapter describes can be looked
at: <kbd>1</kbd> cycles the opaque geometry's cull mode, <kbd>2</kbd> toggles sorting the
panes, <kbd>3</kbd> toggles the panes' depth writes, and <kbd>4</kbd> switches the ferns
between alpha testing and alpha blending.

```cpp title="code/src/4.advanced_vulkan/blending_and_culling/main.cpp"
        glm::vec3(placement.size.x, placement.size.y, 1.0F));

// this camera angle, but the centre is the honest answer and costs nothing.
```

`model_of` is `T · R · S` again, now with a non-uniform scale: `size.x` stretches the quad's
width, `size.y` its height, and `z` stays 1 because a quad has no depth to scale. The normal
matrix is why that matters — a non-uniform scale is exactly the case 2.2's inverse-transpose
exists for, and `normal_matrix_of` already handles it.

`centre_of` is the foot plus half the height: the point the sort measures distance to.

## Culling happens first

`VkPipelineRasterizationStateCreateInfo` carries two fields that decide whether a triangle
is rasterised at all:

```cpp title="code/common/scaffold/src/pipeline.cpp"
.cullMode = cull_mode_,
.frontFace = front_face_,
```

A closed solid shows you only its outside. Every triangle on the far side of a crate is
hidden by a nearer one, so drawing it is wasted work — and it is wasted *early*, because
the hardware can tell which triangles those are from their winding alone, before it
shades a single fragment. That is the whole idea: culling is the cheapest optimisation in
the pipeline because it is the earliest.

Which winding counts as front is `frontFace`, and it is decided from the triangle's
signed area **in framebuffer coordinates** — after the vertex shader, after the
projection, after everything. That last part is where this series has a trap in it.

### Winding is the sign of a determinant

3.1 met winding as the direction a cross product points. On screen there is no third
dimension to point into, and the same idea becomes a signed area. For a triangle with
corners `a`, `b`, `c` in 2D:

```
                 1     | (b − a).x   (c − a).x |
   area(a,b,c) = ─  ·  |                       |
                 2     | (b − a).y   (c − a).y |

               = ½ · ( (b.x − a.x)(c.y − a.y) − (c.x − a.x)(b.y − a.y) )
```

That is the `z` component of 3.1's cross product with both edges laid flat, halved. Its size
is the triangle's area. Its **sign** says which way round the corners go: swap any two of
them and one column of the determinant changes places with the other, which negates it. So
"clockwise" and "counter-clockwise" are not geometric mysteries. They are the two signs of
this number, and `frontFace` says which sign counts as front.

Now apply a linear map `M` to all three corners — any rotation, scale or reflection of the
screen. The edges become `M(b − a)` and `M(c − a)`, and the determinant of a product is the
product of the determinants:

```
   det [ M(b − a)   M(c − a) ]  =  det( M · [ b − a   c − a ] )
                                =  det(M) · det [ b − a   c − a ]
```

So every triangle's signed area is multiplied by the same number, `det(M)`. If that number
is positive, every winding survives. If it is negative, **every winding reverses at once**.

### The projection matrix and `frontFace` are one decision

Every chapter since 1.13 has built its projection like this:

```cpp title="code/src/4.advanced_vulkan/blending_and_culling/main.cpp"
[[nodiscard]] static glm::mat4 build_projection(VkExtent2D extent) {
    const float aspect =
        static_cast<float>(extent.width) / static_cast<float>(extent.height);
    glm::mat4 projection =
        glm::perspective(glm::radians(45.0F), aspect, 0.1F, 100.0F);
    projection[1][1] *= -1.0F;  // Vulkan's clip-space y points down.
    return projection;
}
```

Negating `projection[1][1]` negates every vertex's clip-space `y`. The perspective divide
then divides `x` and `y` by the same positive `w`, and `−y / w = −(y / w)`, so the flip
arrives in normalised device coordinates unchanged. There it is the linear map

```
   M  =  | 1    0 |        det(M)  =  1 · (−1)  −  0 · 0  =  −1
         | 0   −1 |
```

and the previous section says what a determinant of −1 does: it reverses the winding of
every triangle on screen. The viewport transform that follows is a positive scale and a
translation, whose determinant is positive, so nothing after this point undoes it.

That is the whole reason the y-flip and `frontFace` are one decision. It is worth measuring
rather than trusting, because the sample makes it cheap to. Running all four combinations and
counting how much of the frame is left showing the background:

| | `VK_FRONT_FACE_COUNTER_CLOCKWISE` | `VK_FRONT_FACE_CLOCKWISE` |
|---|---|---|
| **y-flip present** | floor drawn (33% background) | floor culled away (89%) |
| **y-flip removed** | floor culled away (89%) | floor drawn (33%) |

The correct value swaps exactly when the flip is removed, as a determinant of −1 says it
must. Neither column is *right* in the abstract — `frontFace` is only meaningful together
with the projection that feeds it, which is why a `frontFace` copied out of OpenGL material,
or out of a Vulkan sample that builds its projection differently, is a coin flip.

The same argument covers something no chapter has done yet. A model matrix with a negative
scale on one axis — a mirrored object — has a negative determinant too, and turns that object
inside out under culling. Exercise 7 asks you to predict which faces you will then see.

Pressing <kbd>1</kbd> cycles the opaque geometry between back, none and front culling.
Culling the front faces is the useful one to look at, because it does not simply delete the
scene — it turns it inside out:

### Not everything has a back

The ferns and the panes are drawn with `VK_CULL_MODE_NONE`, and they have to be. A quad is
a sheet with no inside: whichever way it is wound, the ferns behind the camera's line of
sight are seen from the other side, and culling would delete half of them. Geometry like
this — a surface with two visible faces and one set of triangles — is **two-sided**.

That has a consequence in the shader. A flat sheet has one normal, and seen from behind,
every lighting term built from it is wrong. The fix is to flip it toward the viewer.

## The shader: four entry points that barely differ

The interface blocks, `Globals` and the vertex stage are 4.1's. The push block gains one
comment, because for the first time its `colour.a` means something:

```slang title="code/src/4.advanced_vulkan/blending_and_culling/blending_and_culling.slang"
struct PushConstants {
    float4x4 model;
    float3x3 normal_matrix;
    float4 colour;  // the glass panes carry their transparency in .a
};
[[vk::push_constant]] PushConstants push;

static const float kSpecularStrength = 0.2;

// Anything below this much alpha is not part of the leaf. 0.5 is the conventional
// choice and it is arbitrary: the article works through what moving it does.
static const float kCutoutThreshold = 0.5;
```

4.1's Phong code moves into a function, because three entry points now need it and one of
them needs it two-sided:

```slang title="code/src/4.advanced_vulkan/blending_and_culling/blending_and_culling.slang"
// `two_sided` is what a quad drawn with culling off needs. Its single normal points one
// way; seen from behind, every lighting term built from that normal is wrong. Flipping
// it to face the viewer is the cheap fix, and it is only ever correct for geometry that
// really is a flat sheet -- which is exactly the geometry culling has to be turned off
// for.
float3 shade(VSOutput input, float3 albedo, bool two_sided) {
    float3 normal = normalize(input.normal);
    float3 to_eye = normalize(globals.view_position.xyz - input.world_position);
    if (two_sided && dot(normal, to_eye) < 0.0) {
        normal = -normal;
    }

    float3 to_sun = normalize(-globals.sun_direction.xyz);

    float diffuse_amount = max(dot(normal, to_sun), 0.0);
    float3 diffuse = globals.sun_colour.rgb * diffuse_amount * albedo;

    float3 reflected = reflect(-to_sun, normal);
    float specular_amount = pow(max(dot(to_eye, reflected), 0.0), globals.ambient.w);
    float3 specular = globals.sun_colour.rgb * specular_amount * kSpecularStrength;

    return globals.ambient.rgb * albedo + diffuse + specular;
}
```

`dot(normal, to_eye) < 0` is 2.2's dot product used as a test of which side you are on: the
dot product of two unit vectors is the cosine of the angle between them, and it is negative
exactly when that angle exceeds 90° — when the normal points away from the eye. The function
returns a colour rather than a `float4`, because the three callers disagree about alpha.

The opaque surfaces call it one-sided and write a solid alpha:

```slang title="code/src/4.advanced_vulkan/blending_and_culling/blending_and_culling.slang"
// The opaque surfaces: the floor and the crates. 4.1's shader, with the two-sided flag
// off because a closed box and a floor are never seen from behind.
[shader("fragment")]
float4 fragmentMain(VSOutput input) : SV_Target {
    float3 albedo = base_colour_map.Sample(input.uv).rgb * push.colour.rgb;
    return float4(shade(input, albedo, false), 1.0);
}
```

## Alpha testing is not pipeline state

Vulkan has no alpha-test switch. OpenGL had one and it is gone; the replacement is a branch
in the fragment shader:

```slang title="code/src/4.advanced_vulkan/blending_and_culling/blending_and_culling.slang"
// The ferns, as a cutout.
//
// `discard` ends the invocation: no colour is written, and -- the part that matters --
// no depth is written either. The quad's empty texels leave the depth buffer untouched,
// so whatever is behind them is still drawn. That is what lets an alpha-tested plant be
// treated as ordinary opaque geometry, needing no sorting and no blending.
//
// The cost is that the decision is binary. A texel is in or out, so the silhouette is
// as jagged as the resolution allows, and the sampler's smooth gradient at the leaf's
// edge is thrown away. 4.9 comes back to this with alpha-to-coverage.
[shader("fragment")]
float4 cutoutFragmentMain(VSOutput input) : SV_Target {
    float4 sampled = base_colour_map.Sample(input.uv);
    if (sampled.a < kCutoutThreshold) {
        discard;
    }
    return float4(shade(input, sampled.rgb * push.colour.rgb, true), 1.0);
}
```

`discard` ends the invocation. No colour is written — and, the part that matters, no depth
is written either. The fern's empty texels leave the depth buffer untouched, so whatever is
behind them still gets drawn. That is what lets an **alpha cutout** be treated as ordinary
opaque geometry: depth writes on, blending off, no sorting, drawn in any order.

A survivor writes alpha `1.0`, not `sampled.a`. It was either in or out, and a fragment that
is in is opaque.

:::caution[This needs a device feature, and nothing tells you until it does]
Slang compiles `discard` to `OpDemoteToHelperInvocation` rather than the older `OpKill`,
and that SPIR-V capability has to be requested by name. Nothing in this series had
discarded a fragment before, so nothing had asked for it, and the first run of this
chapter's sample said:

```text
vkCreateShaderModule(): SPIR-V Capability DemoteToHelperInvocation was declared, but one of
the following requirements is required (VkPhysicalDeviceVulkan13Features::shaderDemoteToHelperInvocation
OR VkPhysicalDeviceShaderDemoteToHelperInvocationFeaturesEXT::shaderDemoteToHelperInvocation).
```

The fix is one line in device creation — and it is already in yours. Chapter 1.3 enabled
it, with a comment saying this chapter would be the one to need it:

```cpp title="code/common/scaffold/src/device.cpp"
features13.shaderDemoteToHelperInvocation = VK_TRUE;
```

The shaders still ran on this machine and the ferns still cut out correctly, which is the
point of running with the validation layers on rather than trusting the picture.

*Demote* rather than *kill* is the reason: a demoted invocation stops writing but keeps
running as a helper, so its neighbours in the 2×2 quad the GPU shades together can still
compute the screen-space derivatives that mip selection needs. Killing it outright would
leave those derivatives undefined along every cutout edge.
:::

### What alpha testing costs

The decision is binary: a texel is in or out. The sampler's smooth gradient along the
leaf's edge — the thing that cost you a mip chain and a linear filter — is thrown away, and
the silhouette comes out as hard as the resolution allows.

Press <kbd>4</kbd> to hand the same ferns to the blender instead:

```slang title="code/src/4.advanced_vulkan/blending_and_culling/blending_and_culling.slang"
// The same ferns, handed to the blender instead. No discard, and the sampled alpha goes
// out in the result for the blend equation to use.
//
// This is the version that looks right in a still frame and wrong the moment two plants
// overlap, because blending is order-dependent and these are not sorted. Toggle 4 in the
// sample switches between this entry point and the one above.
[shader("fragment")]
float4 foliageBlendFragmentMain(VSOutput input) : SV_Target {
    float4 sampled = base_colour_map.Sample(input.uv);
    return float4(shade(input, sampled.rgb * push.colour.rgb, true), sampled.a);
}
```

Across the whole frame only 1.90% of pixels change, and they are almost all on the leaf
edges:

## The blend state, field by field

Blending is the last thing that happens to a fragment. The shader has returned a colour,
the depth test has accepted it, and now the hardware has to decide what that colour does to
the one already in the attachment. `VkPipelineColorBlendAttachmentState` is the whole
decision:

```cpp title="code/src/4.advanced_vulkan/blending_and_culling/main.cpp"
const VkPipelineColorBlendAttachmentState source_over{
    .blendEnable = VK_TRUE,
    .srcColorBlendFactor = VK_BLEND_FACTOR_SRC_ALPHA,
    .dstColorBlendFactor = VK_BLEND_FACTOR_ONE_MINUS_SRC_ALPHA,
    .colorBlendOp = VK_BLEND_OP_ADD,
    .srcAlphaBlendFactor = VK_BLEND_FACTOR_ZERO,
    .dstAlphaBlendFactor = VK_BLEND_FACTOR_ONE,
    .alphaBlendOp = VK_BLEND_OP_ADD,
    .colorWriteMask = VK_COLOR_COMPONENT_R_BIT | VK_COLOR_COMPONENT_G_BIT |
                      VK_COLOR_COMPONENT_B_BIT | VK_COLOR_COMPONENT_A_BIT,
};
```

There are **two** equations here, not one — the **blend equation** for colour and a second
for alpha — and each has two **blend factors** and an operation. Both have the same shape:
take the source (what the shader returned), take the destination (what the attachment already
holds), scale each by its factor, and combine them with the op:

```text
result.rgb = src.rgb * SRC_ALPHA + dst.rgb * ONE_MINUS_SRC_ALPHA
result.a   = src.a   * ZERO      + dst.a   * ONE
```

`VK_BLEND_FACTOR_SRC_ALPHA` is the factor `src.a`; `VK_BLEND_FACTOR_ONE_MINUS_SRC_ALPHA` is
`1 − src.a`. `VK_BLEND_OP_ADD` adds the two scaled terms, and it is the op almost every blend
uses — the others are subtract, reverse subtract, min and max.

`colorWriteMask` is a separate gate after all of that: which of the four channels the result
is allowed to touch. All four, here. Clearing a bit is how a pipeline that still has a colour
attachment is kept from writing part of it.

### The over operator

The colour line has a name. Written with `a` for the source's alpha:

```
   C_out  =  a · C_src  +  (1 − a) · C_dst
```

This is the **over operator** — "source over destination" — and it is a linear interpolation
between what was there and what arrived, with the incoming alpha as the weight. At `a = 1`
the new fragment wins outright; at `a = 0` it contributes nothing; at `0.55` the pane
contributes 55% of its own colour and lets 45% of the background through.

That reading — alpha as the fraction of light a layer *blocks* — is what the rest of this
chapter rests on.

### Blending is not commutative

Opaque geometry can be drawn in any order. Whatever arrives second either wins the depth
test or loses it, and the answer does not depend on which arrived first. That freedom is
what the depth buffer buys.

The over operator does not have it. Put two layers `A` and `B` over a background `D`, first
in one order:

```
   A over (B over D)  =  a_A·C_A  +  (1 − a_A)·[ a_B·C_B + (1 − a_B)·D ]
                      =  a_A·C_A  +  (1 − a_A)·a_B·C_B  +  (1 − a_A)(1 − a_B)·D
```

and then in the other, which is the same expression with `A` and `B` exchanged:

```
   B over (A over D)  =  a_B·C_B  +  (1 − a_B)·a_A·C_A  +  (1 − a_A)(1 − a_B)·D
```

Subtract. The background term is identical in both and cancels — whatever is behind the two
layers, it is attenuated by both of them either way. What is left is:

```
   A over (B over D)  −  B over (A over D)
        =  a_A·C_A − a_A·C_A + a_A·a_B·C_A  +  a_B·C_B − a_A·a_B·C_B − a_B·C_B
        =  a_A · a_B · ( C_A − C_B )
```

Three things fall out of that one line:

- The error is **zero only if** one layer is fully transparent (`a = 0`), or the two are
  exactly the same colour. Two real panes of different glass always disagree.
- It is **independent of the background**. The order bug looks the same over the floor, over
  a crate, over the sky.
- Its size is **predictable**. With both alphas at 0.55, `a_A · a_B = 0.3025`, so the wrong
  order is off by about 30% of the difference between the two panes' colours.

That is a strong enough claim to test against the sample. Blending happens in linear colour —
the swapchain is `_SRGB`, so the hardware decodes, blends and re-encodes — so the measurement
decodes the two captures back to linear first. Only two pairs of panes change relative order
when sorting is switched off: red and green are drawn red-first either way, so their overlap
cannot change.

| overlap | predicted `0.3025 · (C_A − C_B)` | measured, sorted − unsorted | pixels |
|---|---|---|---|
| red over blue | (0.212, −0.061, −0.230) | (0.211, −0.061, −0.227) | 11 358 |
| green over blue | (−0.012, 0.121, −0.176) | (−0.012, 0.120, −0.173) | 6 697 |

Every one of the 18 055 pixels that change falls into one of those two rows, within 0.011 of
the prediction — about what 8-bit quantisation of an sRGB value allows. **Order dependence**
is not a vague property of blending; it is `a_A · a_B · (C_A − C_B)`, to the pixel.

:::note[Premultiplied alpha, and why it composes]
The over operator is not commutative, but it *is* associative — `(A over B) over C` equals
`A over (B over C)` — and that is easiest to see in a different representation.

**Premultiplied alpha** stores each colour already multiplied by its own alpha: `C' = a · C`.
Over then becomes

```
   C'_out  =  C'_src  +  (1 − a_src) · C'_dst
   a_out   =  a_src   +  (1 − a_src) · a_dst
```

— the *same* equation for colour and for alpha, with factors `ONE` and `ONE_MINUS_SRC_ALPHA`
on both lines. Expanding `(A over B) over C` and `A over (B over C)` in this form gives
`C'_A + (1 − a_A)·C'_B + (1 − a_A)(1 − a_B)·C'_C` both ways. Associativity is what lets a
renderer flatten a group of layers into one image in advance and composite that image later.

This series stays with straight alpha, where the shader returns the unmultiplied colour. The
panes and ferns are the only blended things in it, and they are blended straight into the
final image.
:::

### The alpha equation is a separate decision, and the obvious answer is wrong

The colour line is the one everybody quotes. The alpha line is the one that bites.

What *should* happen to the destination's alpha when a layer goes over it? Alpha is the
fraction of light a layer blocks. Light gets through two layers only if it gets through
each, so the fraction that gets through both is `(1 − a_src)(1 − a_dst)`, and the fraction
blocked is one minus that:

```
   a_out  =  1 − (1 − a_src)(1 − a_dst)
          =  a_src + a_dst − a_src·a_dst
          =  a_src + a_dst·(1 − a_src)
```

That is **separate alpha blending** done properly: factor `ONE` on the source and
`ONE_MINUS_SRC_ALPHA` on the destination. Now look at the three choices that matter for an
attachment cleared to alpha 1 — the only kind this chapter has:

| alpha factors | equation | `a_out` over `a_dst = 1`, `a_src = 0.55` |
|---|---|---|
| `ONE`, `ONE_MINUS_SRC_ALPHA` | `a_src + a_dst(1 − a_src)` | `0.55 + 0.45 = 1.0` |
| `ZERO`, `ONE` | `a_dst` | `1.0` |
| `ONE`, `ZERO` | `a_src` | **`0.55`**, stored as 140 |

The first two agree whenever the destination is already opaque; the sample uses the second,
which says "leave the target's alpha alone" most directly. The third — "store the alpha that
arrived" — is the obvious-looking one, and it is wrong: it replaces the target's alpha with
the pane's.

It was what this chapter's sample had first, and it is what `vkcommon`'s `alpha_blending()`
helper carried until this chapter found the problem — which is why the version you wrote in
1.7 already has `ZERO` and `ONE`. Every colour on screen was correct, and every blended
fragment was quietly overwriting the attachment's alpha with its own. The table predicts the
exact byte: `0.55 × 255 = 140.25`, stored as 140 — and the reference capture came back with
80038 pixels at alpha 140, in an image that is supposed to be opaque.

The ferns show it more starkly, but only with <kbd>4</kbd> held on the blended mode. The
cut-out shader ends `return float4(shade(...), 1.0)`, so a fern that survives the alpha test
writes a solid alpha; the blended one passes `sampled.a` straight through, and an empty texel
has `sampled.a = 0`. Sampling the same floor pixel under an empty fern texel in each:

```text
cut out (default) = (100, 94, 87, 255)
blended  (key 4)  = (100, 94, 87,   0)
```

Identical colour, alpha zero — exactly `ONE, ZERO` with `a_src = 0`.

Nothing about the rendered frame looked wrong, which is why this survived until a capture
was inspected channel by channel: presenting a swapchain image does not oblige anyone to
read its alpha. It stops being ignorable the moment that image is consumed as data — a PNG
on a web page, or an offscreen target something later samples, which is chapter 4.3. And
there the first row of the table becomes the only right answer, because an offscreen target
cleared to alpha 0 is exactly the case where `ZERO, ONE` would leave it transparent forever.

## Sorting: the painter's algorithm

Blending takes away the freedom opaque geometry had. A transparent surface has to be drawn
*after* everything behind it, and the depth test cannot sort this out, because a pane that is
behind another pane still has to be drawn — and drawn first.

Drawing far things first and near things over them is the **painter's algorithm**, after the
way a painter lays in a background before the figures in front of it. Here it is a sort of
three indices by distance from the eye, farthest first.

The panes are sorted by *squared* distance, `dot(v, v)` rather than `length(v)`. That is safe
because the square root is increasing: for non-negative numbers, `x > y` exactly when
`√x > √y`, so both keys put the panes in the same order and the square root is wasted work.

Press <kbd>2</kbd> to turn the sort off and the panes are drawn in array order instead, which
puts the farthest one last:

<figure>
  <img src="/img/4.2/panes-unsorted.png" alt="The three glass panes drawn unsorted: the blue pane appears in front of the red and green ones despite being furthest away" />
  <figcaption>Unsorted: 1.96% of the frame changes, all of it in the regions where two panes
  overlap. The blue pane is the farthest away and is painted on top of both of the others.</figcaption>
</figure>

1.96% is the 18 055 pixels in the table above, out of 921 600.

### `depthWriteEnable`, and a prediction that turned out to be wrong

4.1 introduced `depthWriteEnable` and said a transparent surface wants it off. The reason is
that a blended surface must not hide what is behind it: if a pane stamped its depth, the
panes further back would be rejected before they could contribute anything.

The sample has a key for it — <kbd>3</kbd> — and the expectation was that pressing it would
visibly break the picture. **It does not. Not one pixel changes: 0.00% of the frame.**

The reason is worth more than the demonstration would have been. With a correct
back-to-front sort, every pane is *already* drawn before anything in front of it, so a
depth value written by one pane is never in a position to reject another. Depth writes have
nothing left to do. Sorting has made them redundant.

They stop being redundant the moment the sort is wrong. Turn both off together and the two
failures compound — the nearer panes write depth first, and the farthest pane is then
rejected wherever they cover it, so it loses the overlap regions entirely instead of merely
being painted in the wrong order:

<figure>
  <img src="/img/4.2/panes-unsorted-depth-writes.png" alt="Unsorted panes with depth writes enabled: the blue pane is clipped away wherever the nearer panes already covered it" />
  <figcaption>Unsorted <em>and</em> writing depth. This differs from the unsorted-only image
  in a further 1.96% of pixels — the same overlap regions again, failing a second way.</figcaption>
</figure>

The honest summary is that `depthWriteEnable = false` is insurance, not the mechanism. The
mechanism is the sort. Turning depth writes off is what keeps a *wrong* sort from failing
twice over, and since a correct sort is expensive and often approximate — per-object, not
per-pixel, and undefined for surfaces that intersect — the insurance is worth carrying.

## The frame, in three passes

Opaque first, then the cutouts, then the blended glass. That ordering is the whole of what
this chapter asks of `on_render`:

```cpp title="code/src/4.advanced_vulkan/blending_and_culling/main.cpp"
    void on_render(const vkc::FrameInfo& frame) override {
        camera_.update(delta_time());

        const size_t slot = frame.frame_number % vkc::kFramesInFlight;
        update_globals(slot, frame.extent);

        vkc::begin_rendering(frame.cmd, frame.view, depth_.view(), frame.extent,
                             {{0.05F, 0.06F, 0.09F, 1.0F}});

        set_viewport(frame);

        vkCmdBindDescriptorSets(frame.cmd, VK_PIPELINE_BIND_POINT_GRAPHICS,
                                pipeline_layout_, 0, 1, &frame_sets_[slot], 0, nullptr);

        // --- opaque, front to back would be ideal and any order is correct ---------
        //
        // Nothing here blends, so the depth test settles every overlap however these
        // are ordered. That freedom is exactly what the transparent passes below lose.
        const VkPipeline opaque = opaque_pipelines_[cull_index_];

        bind_geometry(frame.cmd, quad_vertices_, quad_indices_);
        vkCmdBindPipeline(frame.cmd, VK_PIPELINE_BIND_POINT_GRAPHICS, opaque);
        bind_material(frame.cmd, floor_set_);
        push(frame.cmd, glm::mat4(1.0F), kWhite);
        vkCmdDrawIndexed(frame.cmd, kQuadIndexCount, 1, kFloorFirstIndex, 0, 0);

        bind_geometry(frame.cmd, cube_vertices_, cube_indices_);
        bind_material(frame.cmd, crate_set_);
        for (const Placement& crate : kCrates) {
            // The cube is centred on its origin, so it has to be lifted half its height
            // to stand on the floor rather than be buried to the waist in it.
            push(frame.cmd,
                 glm::translate(model_of(crate), glm::vec3(0.0F, 0.5F, 0.0F)), kWhite);
            vkCmdDrawIndexed(frame.cmd, static_cast<uint32_t>(kCubeIndices.size()), 1, 0,
                             0, 0);
        }
```

vkcommon's `begin_rendering` is back — colour and depth, which is all this chapter needs.
`glm::translate(model_of(crate), ...)` multiplies the translation on the *right*, so it
happens first, in the crate's own frame: lift the cube by half a unit so its bottom face
touches its foot, then place it. On the left it would lift the crate in world space after
rotating it, which here happens to be the same thing, and in general is not.

"Front to back would be ideal" in the comment is about performance, not correctness: an
opaque surface drawn first lets the depth test reject what is behind it before its fragment
shader runs.

The ferns, whichever pipeline <kbd>4</kbd> selects:

```cpp title="code/src/4.advanced_vulkan/blending_and_culling/main.cpp"

        bind_geometry(frame.cmd, quad_vertices_, quad_indices_);
        bind_material(frame.cmd, fern_set_);
        vkCmdBindPipeline(frame.cmd, VK_PIPELINE_BIND_POINT_GRAPHICS,
                          fern_cutout_ ? fern_cutout_pipeline_ : fern_blend_pipeline_);
        for (const Placement& fern : kFerns) {
            push(frame.cmd, model_of(fern), kWhite);
            vkCmdDrawIndexed(frame.cmd, kQuadIndexCount, 1, kUprightFirstIndex, 0, 0);
        }
```

And the glass, sorted:

```cpp title="code/src/4.advanced_vulkan/blending_and_culling/main.cpp"
        // --- the glass ------------------------------------------------------------
        //
        // Blending is not commutative: "draw red over green" and "draw green over red"
        // are different pictures. The depth test cannot rescue the order the way it does
        // for opaque geometry, because a pane that is behind another pane still has to
        // be drawn -- and drawn first.
        std::array<size_t, kPanes.size()> order{};
        for (size_t i = 0; i < order.size(); ++i) {
            order[i] = i;
        }
        if (sort_panes_) {
            const glm::vec3 eye = camera_.position;
            std::sort(order.begin(), order.end(), [&](size_t a, size_t b) {
                // Squared distance is enough: nothing here needs the actual length, and
                // a sort only ever compares.
                return glm::dot(centre_of(kPanes[a]) - eye, centre_of(kPanes[a]) - eye) >
                       glm::dot(centre_of(kPanes[b]) - eye, centre_of(kPanes[b]) - eye);
            });
        }

        vkCmdBindPipeline(frame.cmd, VK_PIPELINE_BIND_POINT_GRAPHICS,
                          pane_depth_writes_ ? pane_depth_write_pipeline_ : pane_pipeline_);
        for (const size_t index : order) {
            push(frame.cmd, model_of(kPanes[index]), kPaneColours[index]);
            vkCmdDrawIndexed(frame.cmd, kQuadIndexCount, 1, kUprightFirstIndex, 0, 0);
        }

        vkCmdEndRendering(frame.cmd);
    }
```

The sort permutes *indices*, not the placements, so the colour array stays paired with its
panes: `kPanes[index]` and `kPaneColours[index]` are always the same pane. The comparator
returns `true` when `a` is farther than `b`, so `std::sort` puts the farthest first — the
painter's order. It runs every frame, because the camera moves and the order it produces can
change as it does.

`push`, `bind_geometry`, `bind_material`, `set_viewport`, `create_uniform_buffers` and
`update_globals` are 4.1's, unchanged.

## Three textures, two samplers

```cpp title="code/src/4.advanced_vulkan/blending_and_culling/main.cpp"
    void load_textures() {
        crate_map_ = vkc::load_texture(
            context(), vkc::asset_path("textures/lvk_crate_diffuse.png"),
            VK_FORMAT_R8G8B8A8_SRGB);
        // Not 1.11's grid, which every chapter up to here has used. That texture is
        // loud by design -- it exists to make filtering artefacts obvious -- and this
        // chapter needs the eye on the glass and the ferns, not on the floor.
        floor_map_ = vkc::load_texture(context(),
                                       vkc::asset_path("textures/lvk_ground.png"),
                                       VK_FORMAT_R8G8B8A8_SRGB);
        // The fern is the first texture in the series whose alpha carries information.
        // Loading it as _SRGB is still right: the sRGB transfer function applies to the
        // three colour channels only, and alpha is left linear by definition.
        fern_map_ = vkc::load_texture(context(),
                                      vkc::asset_path("textures/lvk_foliage.png"),
                                      VK_FORMAT_R8G8B8A8_SRGB);

        sampler_ = vkc::Sampler(context(), vkc::SamplerDesc{});
        // The fern needs CLAMP_TO_EDGE. Its quad's uvs stay inside 0..1, but a sampler
        // filtering near the edge reaches past it, and REPEAT would fetch the opposite
        // side of the leaf and smear a sliver of it around the border.
        clamp_sampler_ = vkc::Sampler(
            context(),
            vkc::SamplerDesc{.address_mode = VK_SAMPLER_ADDRESS_MODE_CLAMP_TO_EDGE});

        context().name(crate_map_.handle(), VK_OBJECT_TYPE_IMAGE, "crate diffuse map");
        context().name(floor_map_.handle(), VK_OBJECT_TYPE_IMAGE, "floor grid map");
        context().name(fern_map_.handle(), VK_OBJECT_TYPE_IMAGE, "fern cutout map");
    }
```

`lvk_ground.png` and `lvk_foliage.png` are this project's own, drawn by
`tools/make_textures.py`. The second sampler is the first time a chapter has needed two:
`SamplerDesc` is 1.11's aggregate with a default for every field, so a designated initialiser
changes the one field that differs.

Why clamping matters here: a linear filter at `u = 0.999` blends the texel there with its
neighbour, and under `REPEAT` the neighbour of the last column is the first. For the grid
floor that is invisible, because the grid tiles. For a fern it would drag a line of the
leaf's opposite edge around the quad's border — and the alpha test would then keep it.

## Seven pipelines

The vertex input, push range and pipeline layout are 4.1's, character for character. The

```cpp title="code/src/4.advanced_vulkan/blending_and_culling/main.cpp"
        const VkShaderModule shader =
            vkc::load_shader(device, LVK_CHAPTER_ID, "blending_and_culling.slang");

        // Spelled-out return type, for 4.1's reason: the setters hand back a reference to

        const auto base = [&]() -> vkc::PipelineBuilder {
            return vkc::PipelineBuilder(device)
                .shaders(shader)
                .vertex_input(std::span(&binding, 1), attributes)
                .colour_attachment(swapchain().format())
                .layout(pipeline_layout_);
        };
```

No `stencil_attachment()`: the rendering instance has no stencil attachment this time, so
the pipelines must not declare one — the mirror image of 4.1's validation error.

The blend state is the `source_over` struct already shown. Then the opaque geometry, three
times, one pipeline per cull mode:

```cpp title="code/src/4.advanced_vulkan/blending_and_culling/main.cpp"
        for (size_t i = 0; i < kCullModes.size(); ++i) {
            opaque_pipelines_[i] =
                base()
                    .cull(kCullModes[i], VK_FRONT_FACE_COUNTER_CLOCKWISE)
                    .depth(depth_format_, /*test=*/true, /*write=*/true)
                    .build();
        }
```

Three pipelines rather than one with dynamic state because cull mode is baked in unless a
pipeline lists `VK_DYNAMIC_STATE_CULL_MODE` — core since Vulkan 1.3, and a single
`vkCmdSetCullMode` away. Baking it keeps the lesson in the pipeline object, where 4.1 put
depth bias.

The quads share a second opening with culling off:

```cpp title="code/src/4.advanced_vulkan/blending_and_culling/main.cpp"
        const auto quad_base = [&]() -> vkc::PipelineBuilder {
            return base().cull(VK_CULL_MODE_NONE, VK_FRONT_FACE_COUNTER_CLOCKWISE);
        };

        fern_cutout_pipeline_ =
            quad_base()
                .shaders(shader, "vertexMain", "cutoutFragmentMain")
                .depth(depth_format_, /*test=*/true, /*write=*/true)
                .build();
```

The cutout ferns are opaque in every respect the pipeline can see: depth test on, depth
writes on, blending off. The transparency is entirely the fragment shader's `discard`, and
the pipeline does not know it is happening.

The blended ferns change two things, and they travel together:

```cpp title="code/src/4.advanced_vulkan/blending_and_culling/main.cpp"
        fern_blend_pipeline_ =
            quad_base()
                .shaders(shader, "vertexMain", "foliageBlendFragmentMain")
                .depth(depth_format_, /*test=*/true, /*write=*/false)
                .colour_blend(source_over)
                .build();
```

Blending on, so the sampled alpha is used rather than thresholded. Depth *writes* off, so a
fern does not stamp its whole rectangle — empty texels included — into the depth buffer and
hide the ferns behind it. Depth *testing* stays on: a transparent surface is still occluded
by the opaque floor and crates in front of it.

The glass, twice — once right, and once with the mistake the <kbd>3</kbd> key shows:

```cpp title="code/src/4.advanced_vulkan/blending_and_culling/main.cpp"
        pane_pipeline_ = quad_base()
                             .shaders(shader, "vertexMain", "flatFragmentMain")
                             .depth(depth_format_, /*test=*/true, /*write=*/false)
                             .colour_blend(source_over)
                             .build();

        pane_depth_write_pipeline_ =
            quad_base()
                .shaders(shader, "vertexMain", "flatFragmentMain")
                .depth(depth_format_, /*test=*/true, /*write=*/true)
                .colour_blend(source_over)
                .build();

        vkDestroyShaderModule(device, shader, nullptr);
```

## What changed since 4.1

- back to `vkcommon`'s depth-only buffer: nothing here tests the stencil
- three cull modes as three pipelines, and the determinant that ties `frontFace` to the
  projection's y-flip
- `discard` in a fragment shader, and the device feature it turns out to require
- the full `VkPipelineColorBlendAttachmentState`, both equations, via `colour_blend()`
- the over operator, its order error `a_A · a_B · (C_A − C_B)`, measured to the pixel
- a back-to-front sort in `on_render`, and the finding that it makes `depthWriteEnable`
  redundant rather than the other way round
- two new textures, `lvk_foliage.png` and `lvk_ground.png`, both from `tools/make_textures.py`
- 27 chapters in the build now

## Words introduced in this chapter

| | |
|---|---|
| **cull mode** | which facing to discard before rasterisation: none, front, back, or both |
| **front face** | which sign of a triangle's screen-space area counts as facing the camera |
| **signed area** | `½·det[b − a, c − a]`; its magnitude is the triangle's area and its sign is the winding |
| **determinant** | the factor a linear map scales every signed area by; negative means it reverses every winding |
| **two-sided geometry** | a surface seen from both sides with one set of triangles — drawn unculled, with the normal flipped toward the eye |
| **alpha cutout** | treating a texel as fully present or fully absent by thresholding its alpha |
| **discard** | ending a fragment invocation so it writes neither colour nor depth |
| **alpha blending** | combining the shader's output with the attachment's existing contents, weighted by alpha |
| **blend factor** | the multiplier on the source or destination term — `SRC_ALPHA`, `ONE_MINUS_SRC_ALPHA`, `ONE`, `ZERO`, … |
| **blend equation** | `src·factor op dst·factor`, once for colour and once for alpha |
| **over operator** | `a·C_src + (1 − a)·C_dst`: a layer that blocks the fraction `a` of what is behind it |
| **order dependence** | swapping two layers changes the result by exactly `a_A·a_B·(C_A − C_B)` |
| **separate alpha blending** | giving alpha its own factors; the physically right one is `a_src + a_dst(1 − a_src)` |
| **premultiplied alpha** | storing `a·C` instead of `C`, which makes colour and alpha obey one associative equation |
| **painter's algorithm** | drawing far things first so nearer ones are blended over them |

## Exercises

1. Set `colorWriteMask` on the pane pipeline to RGB only, drop the alpha equation back to
   `ONE`/`ZERO`, and capture a screenshot. Explain why the image is opaque again, and which
   of the two fixes you would rather ship.
2. Give the three panes three different alphas and predict, from `a_A · a_B · (C_A − C_B)`,
   which overlap region changes most when sorting is turned off. Then check yourself against
   the image.
3. Work out the order error for the triple overlap — all three panes over one pixel, sorted
   against unsorted. Show that the background still cancels. (This camera never shows a
   pixel under all three; move it until it does, and measure.)
4. Make two panes intersect each other in a cross. No per-object sort can order them
   correctly — work out why, and what the smallest unit is that *could* be sorted.
5. Raise `kCutoutThreshold` to 0.9 and lower it to 0.1. One makes the fern thinner and one
   fatter; explain which, in terms of the texture's alpha gradient, and why a mip level
   makes the effect worse at a distance.
6. Blend the ferns *and* give their pipeline depth writes. Predict the artefact before you
   look, then find the camera angle that shows it most clearly.
7. Give one crate a scale of `-1` on x in `model_of`. Its determinant is now negative. Before
   running it, say which of its faces will be culled and what you will see instead.
8. The crates are drawn with back-face culling and are closed boxes. Open one — delete its
   top face from the index buffer — and find the cull mode that makes the inside look right.
