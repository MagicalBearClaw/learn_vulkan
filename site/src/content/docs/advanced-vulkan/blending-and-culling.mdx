4.1 took apart what happens to a fragment's *depth*. This chapter takes apart what happens
to its *colour* — and, before that, the one piece of fixed-function state that runs before
the fragment shader rather than after it.

The scene has three kinds of surface in it. The crates and the floor are opaque. The ferns
look like plants and are flat quads with most of their texture thrown away. The panes are
transparent, and they are the only things here that had to be sorted by hand.

## Culling happens first

`VkPipelineRasterizationStateCreateInfo` carries two fields that decide whether a triangle
is rasterised at all:

```cpp title="code/common/scaffold/src/pipeline.cpp"
.cullMode = cull_mode_,
.frontFace = front_face_,
```

A closed solid shows you only its outside. Every triangle on the far side of a crate is
hidden by a nearer one, so drawing it is wasted work — and it is wasted *early*, because
the hardware can tell which triangles those are from their winding alone, before it
shades a single fragment. That is the whole idea: culling is the cheapest optimisation in
the pipeline because it is the earliest.

Which winding counts as front is `frontFace`, and it is decided from the triangle's
signed area **in framebuffer coordinates** — after the vertex shader, after the
projection, after everything. That last part is where this series has a trap in it.

### The projection matrix and `frontFace` are one decision

Every chapter since 1.13 has built its projection like this:

```cpp title="code/src/4.advanced_vulkan/blending_and_culling/main.cpp"
glm::mat4 projection = glm::perspective(glm::radians(45.0F), aspect, 0.1F, 100.0F);
projection[1][1] *= -1.0F;  // Vulkan's clip-space y points down.
```

Negating one axis mirrors the image, and mirroring an image reverses the apparent winding
of every triangle in it. So that one line changes which `frontFace` value is correct.

This is worth measuring rather than arguing about, because the sample makes it cheap to.
Running all four combinations and counting how much of the frame is left showing the
background:

| | `VK_FRONT_FACE_COUNTER_CLOCKWISE` | `VK_FRONT_FACE_CLOCKWISE` |
|---|---|---|
| **y-flip present** | floor drawn (33% background) | floor culled away (89%) |
| **y-flip removed** | floor culled away (89%) | floor drawn (33%) |

The correct value swaps exactly when the flip is removed. Neither column is *right* in the
abstract — `frontFace` is only meaningful together with the projection that feeds it, which
is why a `frontFace` copied out of OpenGL material, or out of a Vulkan sample that builds
its projection differently, is a coin flip.

Pressing <kbd>1</kbd> cycles the opaque geometry between back, none and front culling.
Culling the front faces is the useful one to look at, because it does not simply delete the
scene — it turns it inside out:

<figure>
  <img src="/img/4.2/cull-front.png" alt="The same scene with front-face culling: the floor has vanished and the crates show their interior faces" />
  <figcaption><code>VK_CULL_MODE_FRONT_BIT</code>: 64.65% of the frame changes. The floor is gone
  because its only face pointed at the camera, and the crates now show the inside of their far
  walls. The ferns are unaffected — their pipeline never culls anything.</figcaption>
</figure>

### Not everything has a back

The ferns and the panes are drawn with `VK_CULL_MODE_NONE`, and they have to be. A quad is
a sheet with no inside: whichever way it is wound, the ferns behind the camera's line of
sight are seen from the other side, and culling would delete half of them.

That has a consequence in the shader. A flat sheet has one normal, and seen from behind,
every lighting term built from it is wrong. The fix is to flip it toward the viewer:

```slang title="code/src/4.advanced_vulkan/blending_and_culling/blending_and_culling.slang"
float3 shade(VSOutput input, float3 albedo, bool two_sided) {
    float3 normal = normalize(input.normal);
    float3 to_eye = normalize(globals.view_position.xyz - input.world_position);
    if (two_sided && dot(normal, to_eye) < 0.0) {
        normal = -normal;
    }
    // ...
```

This is only ever correct for geometry that really is a flat sheet — which is exactly the
geometry culling had to be turned off for. The two decisions travel together.

## Alpha testing is not pipeline state

Vulkan has no alpha-test switch. OpenGL had one and it is gone; the replacement is a branch
in the fragment shader:

```slang title="code/src/4.advanced_vulkan/blending_and_culling/blending_and_culling.slang"
float4 sampled = base_colour_map.Sample(input.uv);
if (sampled.a < kCutoutThreshold) {
    discard;
}
```

`discard` ends the invocation. No colour is written — and, the part that matters, no depth
is written either. The fern's empty texels leave the depth buffer untouched, so whatever is
behind them still gets drawn. That is what lets an alpha-tested plant be treated as
ordinary opaque geometry: depth writes on, blending off, no sorting, drawn in any order.

:::caution[This needs a device feature, and nothing tells you until it does]
Slang compiles `discard` to `OpDemoteToHelperInvocation` rather than the older `OpKill`,
and that SPIR-V capability has to be requested by name. Nothing in this series had
discarded a fragment before, so nothing had asked for it, and the first run of this
chapter's sample said:

```text
vkCreateShaderModule(): SPIR-V Capability DemoteToHelperInvocation was declared, but one of
the following requirements is required (VkPhysicalDeviceVulkan13Features::shaderDemoteToHelperInvocation
OR VkPhysicalDeviceShaderDemoteToHelperInvocationFeaturesEXT::shaderDemoteToHelperInvocation).
```

One line in `vkcommon`'s device creation fixes it:

```cpp title="code/common/scaffold/src/context.cpp"
features13.shaderDemoteToHelperInvocation = VK_TRUE;
```

The shaders still ran on this machine and the ferns still cut out correctly, which is the
point of running with the validation layers on rather than trusting the picture.
:::

### What alpha testing costs

The decision is binary: a texel is in or out. The sampler's smooth gradient along the
leaf's edge — the thing that cost you a mip chain and a linear filter — is thrown away, and
the silhouette comes out as hard as the resolution allows.

Press <kbd>4</kbd> to hand the same ferns to the blender instead. Across the whole frame
only 1.90% of pixels change, and they are almost all on the leaf edges:

<figure>
  <img src="/img/4.2/fern-edge-detail.png" alt="Side-by-side magnification of fern leaflets: the left panel has hard stair-stepped edges, the right has smooth graded ones" />
  <figcaption>The same leaflets at 4×, cut out on the left and blended on the right. In the
  150×112 region where the two differ most, 42.14% of pixels are not the same colour. The
  cutout's edges step; the blended ones keep the gradient the sampler produced.</figcaption>
</figure>

So why not blend the ferns? Because blending costs an ordering constraint, and alpha
testing does not — which is the rest of this chapter.

## The blend state, field by field

Blending is the last thing that happens to a fragment. The shader has returned a colour,
the depth test has accepted it, and now the hardware has to decide what that colour does to
the one already in the attachment. `VkPipelineColorBlendAttachmentState` is the whole
decision:

```cpp title="code/src/4.advanced_vulkan/blending_and_culling/main.cpp"
const VkPipelineColorBlendAttachmentState source_over{
    .blendEnable = VK_TRUE,
    .srcColorBlendFactor = VK_BLEND_FACTOR_SRC_ALPHA,
    .dstColorBlendFactor = VK_BLEND_FACTOR_ONE_MINUS_SRC_ALPHA,
    .colorBlendOp = VK_BLEND_OP_ADD,
    .srcAlphaBlendFactor = VK_BLEND_FACTOR_ZERO,
    .dstAlphaBlendFactor = VK_BLEND_FACTOR_ONE,
    .alphaBlendOp = VK_BLEND_OP_ADD,
    .colorWriteMask = VK_COLOR_COMPONENT_R_BIT | VK_COLOR_COMPONENT_G_BIT |
                      VK_COLOR_COMPONENT_B_BIT | VK_COLOR_COMPONENT_A_BIT,
};
```

There are **two** equations here, not one, and each has two factors and an operation. Both
have the same shape — take the source, take the destination, scale each by its factor,
combine them with the op:

```text
result.rgb = src.rgb * SRC_ALPHA + dst.rgb * ONE_MINUS_SRC_ALPHA
result.a   = src.a   * ZERO      + dst.a   * ONE
```

The colour line is source-over, the arrangement almost everything uses. Written out, it is a
linear interpolation between what was there and what arrived, with the incoming alpha as the
blend weight: at `src.a = 1` the new fragment wins outright, at `0` it contributes nothing.

### The alpha equation is a separate decision, and the obvious answer is wrong

The colour line is the one everybody quotes. The alpha line is the one that bites.

The obvious-looking choice for it — `ONE` and `ZERO`, "store the alpha that arrived" — was
what this chapter's sample had first, and it is what `vkcommon`'s `alpha_blending()` helper
had carried since it was written. Every colour on screen was correct, and every blended
fragment was quietly overwriting the attachment's alpha with its own.

The panes show it in the finished scene, because they blend whatever the toggles say: the
reference capture came back with 80038 pixels at alpha 140 — which is 0.55 × 255, the panes'
own transparency — in an image that is supposed to be opaque.

The ferns show it more starkly, but only with <kbd>4</kbd> held on the blended mode. The
cut-out shader ends `return float4(shade(...), 1.0)`, so a fern that survives the alpha test
writes a solid alpha; the blended one passes `sampled.a` straight through. Sampling the same
floor pixel under an empty fern texel in each:

```text
cut out (default) = (100, 94, 87, 255)
blended  (key 4)  = (100, 94, 87,   0)
```

Identical colour, alpha zero.

Nothing about the rendered frame looked wrong, which is why this survived until a capture
was inspected channel by channel: presenting a swapchain image does not oblige anyone to
read its alpha. It stops being ignorable the moment that image is consumed as data — a PNG
on a web page, or an offscreen target something later samples, which is chapter 4.3.
`ZERO` and `ONE` keep whatever alpha the attachment already had, which for a target cleared
to 1 is what is wanted.

## Blending is not commutative, and the depth test cannot save you

Opaque geometry can be drawn in any order. Whatever arrives second either wins the depth
test or loses it, and the answer does not depend on which arrived first. That freedom is
what the depth buffer buys.

Blending takes it away. "Red over green" and "green over red" are different pictures, so a
transparent surface has to be drawn *after* everything behind it. The depth test cannot
sort this out, because a pane that is behind another pane still has to be drawn — and drawn
first.

So the panes are sorted by hand, farthest first:

```cpp title="code/src/4.advanced_vulkan/blending_and_culling/main.cpp"
if (sort_panes_) {
    const glm::vec3 eye = camera_.position;
    std::sort(order.begin(), order.end(), [&](size_t a, size_t b) {
        return glm::dot(centre_of(kPanes[a]) - eye, centre_of(kPanes[a]) - eye) >
               glm::dot(centre_of(kPanes[b]) - eye, centre_of(kPanes[b]) - eye);
    });
}
```

Press <kbd>2</kbd> to turn that off and the panes are drawn in array order instead, which
puts the farthest one last:

### `depthWriteEnable`, and a prediction that turned out to be wrong

4.1 introduced `depthWriteEnable` and said a transparent surface wants it off. The reason is
that a blended surface must not hide what is behind it: if a pane stamped its depth, the
panes further back would be rejected before they could contribute anything.

The sample has a key for it — <kbd>3</kbd> — and the expectation was that pressing it would
visibly break the picture. **It does not. Not one pixel changes: 0.00% of the frame.**

The reason is worth more than the demonstration would have been. With a correct
back-to-front sort, every pane is *already* drawn before anything in front of it, so a
depth value written by one pane is never in a position to reject another. Depth writes have
nothing left to do. Sorting has made them redundant.

They stop being redundant the moment the sort is wrong. Turn both off together and the two
failures compound — the nearer panes write depth first, and the farthest pane is then
rejected wherever they cover it, so it loses the overlap regions entirely instead of merely
being painted in the wrong order:

<figure>
  <img src="/img/4.2/panes-unsorted-depth-writes.png" alt="Unsorted panes with depth writes enabled: the blue pane is clipped away wherever the nearer panes already covered it" />
  <figcaption>Unsorted <em>and</em> writing depth. This differs from the unsorted-only image
  in a further 1.96% of pixels — the same overlap regions again, failing a second way.</figcaption>
</figure>

The honest summary is that `depthWriteEnable = false` is insurance, not the mechanism. The
mechanism is the sort. Turning depth writes off is what keeps a *wrong* sort from failing
twice over, and since a correct sort is expensive and often approximate — per-object, not
per-pixel, and undefined for surfaces that intersect — the insurance is worth carrying.

:::note[What moved into vkcommon]
One setter on `PipelineBuilder`, for chapters that need a blend mode the boolean does not
cover:

```cpp title="code/common/scaffold/include/vkc/pipeline.hpp"
PipelineBuilder& colour_blend(const VkPipelineColorBlendAttachmentState& state);
```

The struct itself is still written out field by field in this chapter's `main.cpp`. The
builder carries it and decides nothing.

Two fixes went into `vkcommon` alongside it: `shaderDemoteToHelperInvocation` in
`context.cpp`, without which no shader in the series may use `discard`, and the alpha
factors on `alpha_blending()`'s default state, which had the `ONE`/`ZERO` bug described
above. Nothing called that helper yet, so nothing had hit it.
:::

## What changed since 4.1

- back to `vkcommon`'s depth-only buffer: nothing here tests the stencil
- three cull modes as three pipelines, and the measured relationship between `frontFace`
  and the projection's y-flip
- `discard` in a fragment shader, and the device feature it turns out to require
- the full `VkPipelineColorBlendAttachmentState`, both equations, via `colour_blend()`
- a back-to-front sort in `on_render`, and the finding that it makes `depthWriteEnable`
  redundant rather than the other way round
- two new textures, `lvk_foliage.png` and `lvk_ground.png`, both from `tools/make_textures.py`
- 27 chapters in the build now

## Exercises

1. Set `colorWriteMask` on the pane pipeline to RGB only, drop the alpha equation back to
   `ONE`/`ZERO`, and capture a screenshot. Explain why the image is opaque again, and which
   of the two fixes you would rather ship.
2. Give the three panes three different alphas and predict, before running it, which
   overlap regions change when sorting is turned off. Check yourself against the image.
3. Make two panes intersect each other in a cross. No per-object sort can order them
   correctly — work out why, and what the smallest unit is that *could* be sorted.
4. Raise `kCutoutThreshold` to 0.9 and lower it to 0.1. One makes the fern thinner and one
   fatter; explain which, in terms of the texture's alpha gradient, and why a mip level
   makes the effect worse at a distance.
5. Blend the ferns *and* give their pipeline depth writes. Predict the artefact before you
   look, then find the camera angle that shows it most clearly.
6. The crates are drawn with back-face culling and are closed boxes. Open one — delete its
   top face from the index buffer — and find the cull mode that makes the inside look right.
